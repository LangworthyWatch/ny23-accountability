                                         BACKGROUND

      I.          Federal Statutory and Regulatory Framework

           “The Government of the United States has broad, undoubted power over the

subject of immigration and the status of aliens.” Arizona v. United States, 567 U.S. 387,

394 (2012) (citation omitted). “This authority rests, in part, on the National Government’s

constitutional power to ‘establish an uniform Rule of Naturalization.’” Id. at 394–95

(quoting Art. I, § 8, cl. 4). “The foundation of our laws on immigration and naturalization

is the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA), 66 Stat. 163, as amended, 8 U.S.C. § 1101

et seq., which sets out the terms and conditions of admission to the country and the

subsequent treatment of aliens lawfully in the country.” Kansas v. Garcia, 589 U.S. 191,

195 (2020). Two categories of immigration law are relevant to this case: (1) law regarding

administrative warrants and detainers; and (2) law related to information sharing and

cooperation. See Boston, 833 F. Supp. 3d at 35–37; Illinois, 796 F. Supp. 3d at 505.

                  a. Administrative Warrants and Immigration Detainers

       To facilitate enforcement of the United States’ immigration law, federal officers may

effectuate arrests pursuant to a warrant issued by the Attorney General, i.e. an

administrative warrant. 8 U.S.C. § 1226(a). Further, federal immigration officers can

request assistance from any other federal, state, or local law enforcement agency that

has custody of an individual the Department of Homeland Security (“DHS” or the

“Department”) seeks to arrest and remove by issuing a section 287.7 detainer. 8 C.F.R.

§ 287.7(a). A section 287.7 detainer has two components. First, a “request that such

agency advise the Department, prior to release of the alien.” Id. Second, a request that

“such agency shall maintain custody of the alien” for up to forty-eight hours. Id. at (d).

Compliance with section 287.7 detainers is not mandatory. See Hernandez v. United

States, 939 F.3d 191, n.11 (2d Cir. 2019); Orellana v. Cnty. of Suffolk, No. 17-CV-4267

(WFK), 2025 WL 481723, at *2 (E.D.N.Y. Jan. 2, 2025). Federal immigration officers may

detain any non-citizen awaiting a removal decision from immigration court. 8 U.S.C. §

1226(a). Federal immigration officers must detain “criminal aliens” (as defined under id.

§ 1226(c)) and non-citizens subject to a final order of removal during the pendency of the

“removal period” (as defined under id. § 1231(a)(1)–(2)).

                  b. Information Sharing and Cooperation

       “Consultation between federal and state officials is an important feature of the

immigration system.” Arizona, 567 U.S. at 411. To that end, federal law bars federal,

state, and local governmental entities and officials from “prohibit[ing]” or “in any way

restrict[ing]” the flow of “information regarding the citizenship or immigration status, lawful

or unlawful, of any individual” to and from DHS. 8 U.S.C. § 1373(a); see also id. § 1373(b)

(similar); id. § 1644 (similar). Many federal courts have found, “the phrase ‘information

regarding the citizenship or immigration status, lawful or unlawful, of any individual’ is

                                      LEGAL STANDARD

          A defendant may move to dismiss a civil action for “failure to state a claim upon

which relief can be granted.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 12(b)(6). To survive a motion to dismiss for

failure to state a claim, the complaint must contain “enough facts to state a claim to relief

that is plausible on its face.” Bell Atl. Corp. v. Twombly, 550 U.S. 544, 570 (2007); Ashcroft

v. Iqbal, 556 U.S. 662, 697 (2009). “A claim has facial plausibility when the plaintiff pleads

factual content that allows the court to draw the reasonable inference that the defendant

is liable for the misconduct alleged.” Iqbal, 556 U.S. at 678. Bare conclusory statements

will not suffice. Id. at 681. When courts consider whether a complaint “states a plausible

claim for relief,” they must accept all factual allegations stated in the complaint as true

and draw all reasonable inferences in the plaintiff’s favor. Id. at 679; see also Erickson v.

Pardus, 551 U.S. 89, 94 (2007) (per curium).


                                         DISCUSSION

     I.          The United States Lacks Article III Standing

          Article III standing is a “threshold question in every federal case” that “determine[s]

the power of the court to entertain the suit.” Warth v. Seldin, 422 U.S. 490, 498 (1975). “If

plaintiffs lack Article III standing, a court has no subject matter jurisdiction to hear their

claim.” Mahon v. Ticor Title Ins. Co., 683 F.3d 59, 62 (2d Cir. 2012) (citation omitted). The

United States, as the party invoking federal jurisdiction, bears the burden of establishing

the “irreducible constitutional minimum” of standing for each claim asserted. Spokeo, Inc.

v. Robins, 578 U.S. 330, 338 (2016) (citation omitted). “Standing is not dispensed in

gross . . . to the contrary, a plaintiff must demonstrate standing for each claim he seeks

to press and for each form of relief that is sought.’” Town of Chester, N.Y. v. Laroe Ests.,



                                                 8
Case 1:25-cv-04084-RER-PK        Document 79 Filed 10/15/26           Page 9 of 25 PageID #:
                                      <pageID>



Inc., 581 U.S. 433, 439 (2017) (citation modified). To establish standing, “plaintiff must

have (1) suffered an injury in fact, (2) that is fairly traceable to the challenged conduct of

the defendant, and (3) that is likely to be redressed by a favorable judicial decision.”

Spokeo, 578 U.S. at 338 (citations omitted).

       A. 287.7 Immigration Detainers and Administrative Warrants

       One of the United States’ purported injuries is New York City’s non-adherence to

section 287.7 detainer requests that “such agency shall maintain custody of the alien” for

up to forty-eight hours. 8 C.F.R. § 287.7(d). The United States alleges, “[b]y . . . barring

DHS access to aliens in local custody upon their release as provided by federal law (e.g.,

an administrative warrant), New York City’s Sanctuary Provisions require federal

immigration officers to engage in difficult and dangerous efforts to re-arrest aliens who

were previously in local custody, endangering immigration officers, the particular alien,

and others who may be nearby.” (Compl. ¶ 95). The United States asks this Court to

“issue a permanent injunction that prohibits Defendants as well as their successors,

agents, and employees, from enforcing N.Y.C. Administrative Code §§ 9-131; 14-154; 9-

205.” (Compl. at 36).

       “For standing purposes, redressability refers to a ‘non-speculative likelihood that

the injury can be remedied by the requested relief.’” Guan v. Mayorkas, 530 F. Supp. 3d

237, 262–63 (E.D.N.Y. 2021) (citation omitted). “Plaintiffs’ injuries are redressable if their

requested relief can compensate them for their losses or eliminate any effects caused by

the defendants’ challenged conduct.” Id. (citation modified). A “partial remedy satisfies

the redressability requirement.” Uzuegbunam v. Preczewski, 592 U.S. 279, 291 (2021).

        Here, New York law forecloses relief. “As a general rule, it is not a crime for a

 removable alien to remain present in the United States.” Arizona, 567 U.S. at 407. New

 York law does not authorize state and local law enforcement officers to “effectuate arrests

 for civil law immigration violations.” Wells, 168 A.D.3d at 34. Further, New York City

 officers are not empowered to make arrests pursuant to administrative warrants. Id. at 42.

 Thus, even if this Court eliminated sections 9-131(b), 9-205(b), 14-154(b) of New York

 City’s Administrative Code, New York City officers would still be unable to execute a

 section 287.7 detainer request to maintain custody of a non-citizen without a judicial

 warrant.

        As in Boston, “an order enjoining” the City of New York “from enforcing” its

 “prohibition on detaining noncitizens solely pursuant to a civil immigration detainer or

 administrative warrant—could not alter this status quo.” Boston, 833 F. Supp. 3d at 43.

 Other actors, not this Court, hold the authority to grant the United States the redress it

 seeks. The New York legislature “could enact a statute authorizing . . . law enforcement

 officers to arrest people pursuant to federal civil immigration detainers . . . [o]r Congress

 (but not this Court) could enact federal legislation granting local law enforcement officers

 this authority.” See id. at 44. Therefore, the United States’ claims regarding New York

 City’s execution of section 287.7 detainer custody requests are not redressable by the

 relief it seeks, and it therefore lacks standing to challenge the same.

        The United States seeks “a judgment declaring that the challenged provisions

 (N.Y.C. Administrative Code §§ 9-131 and 14-154; 9-205; 10-178 and NYPD Ops. Order

 No. 4) violate 8 U.S.C. § 1373 and are therefore invalid.” (Compl. at 36). “The traceability

 requirement for Article III standing means that the plaintiff must demonstrate a causal

 nexus between the defendant's conduct and the injury.” Chevron Corp. v. Donziger, 833

 F.3d 74, 121 (2d Cir. 2016) (citation modified). As in Boston, “[a]ny injury the United States

 immigration authorities exempt information regarding a person’s legal classification under

 federal law. Section 9-131(h)(1) of the NYC Administrative Code prevents Department of

 Correction personnel from disclosing information to federal immigration authorities in

 United States does not have standing to bring its related claims.

         II.     The United States’ Preemption Claims Fail

           Even if the United States has standing—and it does not—its preemption claims

 fail.

           When courts are called upon to address preemption questions, “the analysis

 always begins with the assumption that the historic police powers of the States are not to

 be superseded by the Federal Act unless that was the clear and manifest purpose of

 Congress.” Island Park, LLC v. CSX Transp., 559 F.3d 96, 101 (2d Cir. 2009) (citation

 modified). “A state’s ability to control its officers and employees lies at the heart of state

 sovereignty.” Illinois, 796 F. Supp. 3d at 523; see also Printz v. United States, 521 U.S.

 898, 931 (1997) (“To say that the Federal Government cannot control the State, but can

 control all of its officers, is to say nothing of significance.”). Further, “[t]he regulation of

 health and safety matters is primarily and historically a matter of local concern.”




                                                12
Case 1:25-cv-04084-RER-PK         Document 79 Filed 10/15/26           Page 13 of 25 PageID #:
                                        <pageID>



 Hillsborough Cnty., Fla. v. Automated Med. Lab’ys, Inc., 471 U.S. 707, 719 (1985). Since

 quintessential police powers are implicated here, a presumption against preemption

 applies.

        All of the United States’ preemption claims—express, conflict, and field—fail

 because the relevant federal statutes do not regulate private actors. In order for a federal

 law to preempt state law, “it must satisfy two requirements . . . [f]irst, it must represent the

 exercise of a power conferred on Congress by the Constitution; pointing to the Supremacy

 Clause will not do” and “[s]econd, since the Constitution ‘confers upon Congress the

 power to regulate individuals, not States,’ . . . the . . . provision at issue must be best read

 as one that regulates private actors.” Murphy v. Nat’l Collegiate Athletic Ass’n, 584 U.S.

 453, 477 (2018) (citation omitted). The United States alleges sections 1373(a) and 1644

 have preemptive effect. (Compl. ¶ 105). Sections 1373(a) and 1644 contain nearly

 identical language and therefore will be analyzed together.

        Neither section 1373(a) nor section 1644 “confers on private entities . . . a federal

 right to engage in certain conduct subject only to certain (federal) constraints.” Murphy,

 584 U.S. at 479. Facially, “[t]he plain text of § 1373 only operates on State and local

 government entities and officials.” Illinois, 796 F. Supp. 3d at 521. In effect, sections

 1373(a) and 1644 regulate “how information ‘regarding citizenship or immigration status’

 of a private individual might be distributed between governments”; they do not “create or

 restrict the rights of any private actor.” Id. Numerous federal courts have found that

 sections 1373(a) and 1644 do not regulate private actors and therefore lack preemptive

 effect. See id.; Colorado v. United States Dep’t of Just., 455 F. Supp. 3d 1034, 1059 (D.

 Colo. 2020); Cnty. of Ocean v. Grewal, 475 F. Supp. 3d 355, 372 (D.N.J. 2020).

Case 1:25-cv-04084-RER-PK        Document 79 Filed 10/15/26         Page 14 of 25 PageID #:

        The United States argues the proper question “is whether the INA as a whole

 regulates private actors.” (Pl.’s. Opp’n at 27). The Court disagrees. The United States’

 proposed lens would allow the federal government to “smuggle unconstitutional

 commands to States into specific provisions of a broader statute, and then rely on

 separate provisions for cover.” Illinois, 796 F. Supp. 3d at 521–22. This approach was

 rejected in Murphy where the Court focused its analysis on the challenged section of the

 Professional and Amateur Sports Protection Act (PAPSA), despite recognizing a “closely

 related provision of PASPA, § 3702(2) does restrict private conduct.” Murphy, 584 U.S.

 at 480. The United States’ argument rests exclusively on commentary from a footnote in

 State of New York v. Department of Justice. (Pl.’s Opp’n at 27 (citing New York v. Dep’t

 of Just., 951 F.3d 84, n. 27 (2d Cir. 2020))). New York analyzed section 1373 as applied

 to a federal funding requirement and did not conclusively address the preemption issue

 raised here, accordingly the Court respectfully declines to follow its dicta. Since sections

 1373(a) and 1644 do not regulate private actors, they do not preempt the challenged

 provisions.

        Notwithstanding the foregoing, the United States’ preemption arguments fail for

 other reasons.

        A. Express Preemption

        “Express preemption arises when a federal statute expressly directs that state law

 be ousted.” Island Park, 559 F.3d at 101 (citation modified). The United States claims

 “federal immigration law’s protections for information sharing . . . [e].g. 8 U.S.C. §§

 1373(a), 1644” expressly preempt the challenged provisions. (Compl. ¶ 105). The United

 States fails to establish that sections 1373(a) and 1644 “oust” the challenged information

Case 1:25-cv-04084-RER-PK        Document 79 Filed 10/15/26         Page 15 of 25 PageID #:
                                       <pageID>



 sharing provisions, nor could they. These statutes only apply “[n]otwithstanding any other

 provision of Federal, State, or local law.” 8 U.S.C. §§ 1373(a), 1644. Against the police

 power presumption and absent clear language, the United States’ express preemption

 claims fail.

        B. Field Preemption

        New York City notes that the complaint “contains one generic reference to

 ‘occupy[ing] the field’ in paragraph 105. However, Plaintiff does not even clearly identify

 what the field is.” (Defs.’ Mem. at 27). The United States counters that through the INA,

 “Congress intended to occupy the field of apprehension and detention of aliens, but

 Defendants’ enactment of the Sanctuary Provisions attempts to invade and regulate

 within this field.” (Pl.’s Opp’n at 28; see also Compl. ¶ 29). Irrespective of whether the

 United States sufficiently pled field preemption, the challenged provisions do not regulate

 the apprehension and detention of non-citizens. The challenged provisions govern the

 allocation of municipal resources towards federal immigration enforcement. Since the

 challenged provisions and the INA occupy different fields, the United States’ field

 preemption claim fails.

        C. Conflict Preemption

        Conflict preemption is present where “‘compliance with both federal and state

 regulations is a physical impossibility” or “the challenged state law stands as an obstacle

 to the accomplishment and execution of the full purposes and objectives of Congress.”

 Arizona, 567 U.S. at 399 (citation modified). “‘The burden of establishing obstacle

 preemption, like that of impossibility preemption, is heavy: the mere fact of tension

      III.        The Tenth Amendment’s Anticommandeering Doctrine Precludes Relief

         Even were the Court to find that the United States has standing and the challenged

 provisions are preempted—both answered in the negative above—United States’ claims

 would still fail because the anticommandeering doctrine of the Tenth Amendment to the

 Constitution of the United States precludes relief.

         “The powers not delegated to the United States by the Constitution, nor prohibited

 by it to the States, are reserved to the States respectively, or to the people.” U.S. Const.

 amend. X.

         The legislative powers granted to Congress are sizable, but they are not
         unlimited. The Constitution confers on Congress not plenary legislative
         power but only certain enumerated powers. Therefore, all other legislative
         power is reserved for the States, as the Tenth Amendment confirms. And
         conspicuously absent from the list of powers given to Congress is the power
         to issue direct orders to the governments of the States. The
         anticommandeering doctrine simply represents the recognition of this limit
         on congressional authority.

 Murphy, 584 U.S. at 471. 4




 4 Adherence     to anticommandeering is important for three reasons. First, “a healthy balance of power
 between the States and the Federal Government reduces the risk of tyranny and abuse from either front.’”

 only because it has been commanded to do so by Congress, responsibility is blurred.” Id. at 473–74. Third,
 it “prevents Congress from shifting the costs of regulation to the States.” Id. at 474. States are tasked with
 “govern[ing] in accordance with the will of their citizens” and “[t]oday, as at the time of the founding, the
 allocation of scarce resources among competing needs and interests lies at the heart of the political
 process.” Alden v. Maine, 527 U.S. 706, 751 (1999).

     IV.         The United States’ Intergovernmental Immunity Claims Fail

 ‘regulat[e] the United States directly or discriminat[e] against the Federal Government or

 II), 596 U.S. 832, 838–39 (2022) (quoting North Dakota v. United States, 495 U.S. 423,

 435 (1990) (plurality opinion) (other citations omitted).

                   a. Direct Regulation

        The Court has “decisively rejected the argument that any state regulation which

 indirectly regulates the Federal Government’s activity is unconstitutional.” North Dakota,

 495 U.S. at 434–35 (citations omitted). “The Court has more recently adopted a functional

 approach to claims of governmental immunity, accommodating of the full range of each

 sovereign’s legislative authority and respectful of the primary role of Congress in resolving

 conflicts between the National and State Governments.” Id. at 435.

        The United States argues New York City’s enforcement of the challenged

 provisions “effects direct regulation of the Federal Government” . . . “[b]y refusing to honor

 civil detainers and warrants expressly authorized by Congress,” “restricting access to

 detention facilities and the aliens within them,” and “limit[ing] the circumstances in which

 federal immigration authorities may interrogate illegal aliens.” (Compl. ¶¶ 112–14).

        The parameters of direct regulation is an open question in this Circuit. New York

 City advocates this Court adhere to McHenry County. “As the Seventh Circuit has

 explained, while ‘States may not regulate the federal government directly,’ a regulation

 prohibiting state or local government cooperation with the federal government ‘regulates

 only State and local entities and law enforcement—not the federal government.’” (Defs.’

 Mem. at 33 (citing McHenry Cnty. v. Kwame Raoul, 44 F.4th 581, 592–93 (7th Cir. 2022)).

 Whereas the United States propones this Court follow CoreCivic. (Pl.’s Opp’n at 20). In

 CoreCivic, the Third Circuit rejected New Jersey’s assertion that “a law regulates the

 federal government directly only if the law’s text applies to it” and that in effect the

 challenged provision “carries the same sting as a law whose text applies expressly to the

 federal government.” CoreCivic, Inc. v. Governor of New Jersey, 145 F.4th 315, 321–22

Case 1:25-cv-04084-RER-PK        Document 79 Filed 10/15/26          Page 22 of 25 PageID #:

 (3d Cir. 2025). Since neither case binds, the Court looks to the doctrines “constitutional

 foundations” and “the actual workings of our federalism.” United States v. New Mexico,

 455 U.S. 720, 731 (1982).

       The intergovernmental immunity doctrine originates from McCulloch v. Maryland.

 In McCulloch the Court held that under the Supremacy Clause, “[t]he states have no

 power, by taxation or otherwise, to retard, impede, burden, or in any manner control the

 operations of the constitutional laws enacted by congress to carry into effect the powers

 vested in the national government.” McCulloch v. State, 17 U.S. 316, 317 (1819).

       Originally we understood it as barring any state law whose effect . . . was or
       might be to increase the cost to the Federal Government of performing its
       functions, including laws that imposed costs on federal contractors. We later
       came to understand the doctrine, however, as prohibiting state laws that
       either regulate the United States directly or discriminate against the Federal
       Government or those with whom it deals. (e.g., contractors).

 United States v. Washington, 596 U.S. 832, 838 (2022) (citation modified).

       One obvious and natural constraint of the intergovernmental immunity doctrine is

 the anti-commandeering doctrine.

       Although the anticommandeering principle is simple and basic, it did not
       emerge in our cases until relatively recently . . . The pioneering case was New
       York. . . Justice O’Connor’s opinion for the Court traced this rule to the basic
       structure of government established under the Constitution. The Constitution,
       she noted, “confers upon Congress the power to regulate individuals, not
       States.” In this respect, the Constitution represented a sharp break from the
       Articles of Confederation. “Under the Articles of Confederation, Congress
       lacked the authority in most respects to govern the people directly.” Instead,
       Congress was limited to acting “only upon the States” . . . The Constitutional
       Convention considered plans that would have preserved this basic structure,
       but it rejected them in favor of a plan under which “Congress would exercise
       its legislative authority directly over individuals rather than over States” . . .
       “We have always understood that even where Congress has the authority
       under the Constitution to pass laws requiring or prohibiting certain acts, it
       lacks the power directly to compel the States to require or prohibit those acts.”
       . . . “Congress may not simply commandee[r] the legislative processes of the

       States by directly compelling them to enact and enforce a federal regulatory
       program.”

 Murphy, 584 U.S. at 471–72 (2018) (citation modified) (quoting New York v. United

 States, 505 U.S. 144, 162–66, 170 (1992)).

       “At least to some degree, the doctrines are opposite sides of the same coin . . . a

 finding that any of the challenged provisions violates intergovernmental immunity would

 imply that a Defendant must assist with the federal government’s immigration

 enforcement effort.” Minnesota, 2026 WL 2085701 at *22; see also California, 921 F.3d

 at 891 (“A finding that SB 54 violates the doctrine of intergovernmental immunity would

 imply that California cannot choose to discriminate against federal immigration authorities

 by refusing to assist their enforcement efforts—a result that would be inconsistent with

 the Tenth Amendment and the anticommandeering rule.”)

       This Court declines to define the bounds of direct regulation here but finds that

 enjoining the challenged provisions would result in the commandeering of state and local

 resources. Such a holding would not “accommodate the full range of each sovereign’s

 legislative authority.” North Dakota, 495 U.S. at 435 (citation modified). Therefore, the

 challenged provisions cannot be classified as functionally regulating the United States.

                  b. Discrimination

       “[A] state law is . . . no longer unconstitutional just because it indirectly increases

 costs for the Federal Government, so long as the law imposes those costs in a neutral,

 nondiscriminatory way.” Washington II, 596 U.S. at 839. “The State does not discriminate

 against the Federal Government and those with whom it deals unless it treats someone

 else better than it treats them.” Washington v. United States (Washington I), 460 U.S.

 536, 544–45 (1983) (citation omitted).

Case 1:25-cv-04084-RER-PK         Document 79 Filed 10/15/26           Page 24 of 25 PageID #:

        The United States argues that Washington I and North Dakota “did not create a

 comparator requirement, but simply made the common-sense observation that the

 Federal Government cannot be said to experience discrimination when it receives more

 favorable treatment than someone else.” (Pl.’s Opp’n at 24). The United States is wrong.

 “Differential treatment is critical to a discrimination-based intergovernmental immunity

 claim.” New York, 814 F. Supp. 3d at 282 (citation modified) (citing McHenry Cnty., 44

 F.4th at 594). “[A] comparator is necessary.” New York, 810 F. Supp. 3d at 353. The

 United States discrimination claim fails for lack of a comparator.

        The United States does not and cannot identify a similarly situated entity. State

 and local agencies and their officers do not enforce immigration law, nor do members of

 the public. (Pl.’s Opp’n at 22). As the United States concedes, the Federal Government

 holds the “exclusive charge of enforcing immigration law.” (Id. at 23). Not only are other

 federal law enforcement agencies with different charges differently situated, “[f]or a state

 law to unconstitutionally discriminate against the federal government, it must treat

 comparable classes of federal and non-federal employees differently, not different

 classes of federal employees.” Illinois, 796 F. Supp. 3d at 534 (citing Washington I, 596

 U.S. at 839) (citation omitted). “[T]he fact that a law affects an exclusively federal domain

 is not evidence of discrimination,” rather “failure to identify a comparator ends the inquiry.”

 New York, 810 F. Supp. 3d at 353.

        In alignment with other federal courts, this Court finds that New York City’s

 provisions that single out immigration enforcement do not discriminate against the federal

 government. See, e.g. Illinois, 796 F. Supp. 3d at 533–34; Minnesota, 2026 WL 2085701,

 at *19–22.
