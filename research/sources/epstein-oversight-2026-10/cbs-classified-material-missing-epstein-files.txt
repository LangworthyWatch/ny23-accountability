                        Coding system reveals missing pages
                        A CBS News analysis of a numerical coding system used by the DOJ to
                        organize the Epstein ﬁles oﬀers clues about some of the documents
                        the department has withheld.

                        The codes don't reveal what's missing, but they do show where gaps
                        exist, raising questions about the DOJ's explanation for why
                        documents were withheld.

                        Among the missing documents are pages from Epstein's own ﬁnancial
                        records collected as evidence in a federal prosecution.

                        Here's how it works: Documents from the Epstein ﬁles are labeled
                        with a string of numbers beginning with the letters EFTA stamped on
                        the bottom right-hand corner; EFTA stands for Epstein Files
                        Transparency Act.




                        An evidence photo from the Epstein files, with its number circled in red in

                        Those are called Bates numbers — a common way of organizing
                        massive document batches in legal cases. Every page receives its own
                        number. In the Epstein ﬁles, the sequence goes from 1 to 2,858,498,
                        which is the tally of approximately how many pages of documents
                        were released.




                        According to the DOJ, other documents — including court records and
                        investigative ﬁles they've previously released — bring the total to over
                        3 million.

                        Alongside the Epstein ﬁles' Bates numbers are other, older coding
                        numbers that appear on many documents. This indicates that those
                        documents had previously been used in federal legal cases, potentially
                        collected as part of an earlier subpoena.

                        For instance, Bates coding on some of the documents is from the
                        Ghislaine Maxwell criminal case and was used to organize ﬁles given
                        to Maxwell's legal team ahead of her 2021 trial as part of the discovery
                        process.

                        Other Bates numbers indicate documents were collected from
                        Epstein's banks, including JPMorgan and Deutsche Bank, during the
                        FBI's investigation into his traﬃcking activities.

                        "De-duping"
                        There is one possible explanation for many of the email gaps.
                        Instructions given to DOJ attorneys working on processing the
                        redactions state:

                        "If the document is an email chain and the most-inclusive email (i.e.,
                        containing all the back-and-forth) has already been reviewed and
                        redacted as appropriate for production, the interstitial back-and-forth
                        can be marked as duplicates."

                        In other words, all emails exchanged prior to the last email in a chain
                        can be considered a duplicate, because the last one should include all
                        of the messages, and eliminating the duplicates will speed up the
                        redaction process. Once a document is marked as a duplicate, it would
                        likely be excluded from the ﬁles.

                        If that was the case, this was only partially accomplished because
                        duplicate emails still exist throughout the ﬁles.

                        There's also the possibility that errors in the process could have
                        resulted in crucial emails mistakenly being deleted.

                        Lack of transparency
                        The review and redaction process was conducted by hundreds of DOJ
                        attorneys working under a compressed timeline. Given the scale of the
                        task, it's possible that pages were withheld as a result of honest
                        mistakes. But without a roadmap to the redactions and removals, it's
                        impossible to make that determination.




                        Independent journalist Katie Phang is suing the Justice Department,
                        seeking the release of more ﬁles as well as an explanation of
                        redactions and removals. A federal judge has made several rulings in
                        her favor and the DOJ is appealing those rulings.

                        As part of her lawsuit, Phang is asking the court to appoint a special
                        master to review the ﬁles.

                        "If it wasn't intentional, then was it gross negligence? Were you just
                        lazy? If it was intentional then I think there's [expletive] there that
                        they don't want people to see," Phang told CBS News. "My speculation
                        runs an entire spectrum of reaction — but at the end of the day it just
                        makes the DOJ look bad."

                        The DOJ has acknowledged making several mistakes.

                        It admitted to removing, and then restoring, a photo of now
                        Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick on a 2012 visit to Epstein's
                        island, contradicting claims Lutnick had previously made about his
                        contacts with Epstein.

                        Last year, DOJ oﬃcials claimed a minute was missing from prison
                        security video from the night Epstein died, which turned out to not be
                        true.

                        "This is a case where the process has inhibited public trust in what is
                        ultimately released, and there's a long tradition of this, whether it's the
                        Pentagon Papers or the Epstein ﬁles," said attorney Stanley Brand, a
                        veteran litigator and distinguished fellow in law and government at
                        Penn State Dickinson Law.




                        "This is just another piece where the government process, where
                        making this stuﬀ available just fuels suspicion that there's something
                        we're not seeing, and I don't know that ever gets resolved."
