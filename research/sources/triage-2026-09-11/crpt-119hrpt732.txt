                          Purpose and Summary
    H.J. Res. 1, introduced by Rep. Andy Biggs (R-AZ), proposes 
an amendment to the Constitution to fix the size of the Supreme 
Court at nine justices. This amendment is designed to insulate 
the Supreme Court from political pressure and maintain its 
integrity, ensuring that it can continue to safeguard the 
rights of all Americans.
                Background and Need for the Legislation
    The Committee on the Judiciary has advocated strongly 
against the expansion of the Supreme Court for political 
purposes, or ``court packing,'' and other efforts to change the 
structure of the Supreme Court in a way that weakens its 
integrity.\1\ In the 119th Congress, the Committee and the 
Subcommittee on Courts, Intellectual Property, Artificial 
Intelligence, and the Internet heard testimony from legal 
experts and historians that outlined the threat to judicial 
integrity posed by court-packing and related proposals.\2\ 
While the number of Supreme Court justices is not specified in 
the Constitution, Congress has kept the number at nine since 
1869.\3\ A recent flurry of partisan expansion proposals 
threaten to undermine the Court,\4\ making it necessary to fix 
the number of justices at nine via constitutional amendment.

The Supreme Court is the Last Line of Defense for Protecting the Rights 
                     and Liberties of All Americans
    Article III of the U.S. Constitution establishes the 
Supreme Court of the United States as the highest court in the 
nation, with final say over disputes involving the Constitution 
and federal law.\5\ The Founders envisioned that the Supreme 
Court would have the power of ``judicial review,'' meaning the 
power to invalidate laws and government actions that violate 
the U.S. Constitution.\6\ As Alexander Hamilton wrote in the 
Federalist No. 78, ``limitations [on Federal power] can be 
preserved in practice in no other way than through the medium 
of courts of justice, whose duty it must be to declare all acts 
contrary to the manifest tenor of the Constitution void.''\7\ A 
court with the power of judicial review could uphold the 
Constitution as our ``highest law,'' protecting the separation 
of powers and our core constitutional liberties from the whims 
of bare and fleeting majorities.\8\ By design, the Founders 
ensured that only large, durable majorities could reshape the 
nature of the nation's social contract.\9\ While the Supreme 
Court's success in protecting individual liberty has been 
uneven throughout history,\10\ key decisions from the Court 
safeguard free speech,\11\ religious freedom,\12\ the right to 
bear arms,\13\ equal treatment under law,\14\ and other core 
American principles.

    In practice, this arrangement makes the Court a ``counter-
majoritarian'' institution,\15\ which is what the Founders 
envisioned.\16\ The Court's role is to keep the country 
tethered to our founding principles, even when doing so is 
politically unpalatable or unpopular.\17\ The Court, in other 
words, exists to defend the ideals of the Declaration of 
Independence and principles of the Constitution against those 
who claim they have found something ``more modern.''\18\ 
Proposals to expand the size of the Supreme Court and ``pack'' 
the Court with justices that would be expected to reliably vote 
for certain policy outcomes would undermine the Court's 
fundamental duty.\19\ Moreover, court packing would likely set 
off an arms race, with each party expanding or contracting the 
Supreme Court to ensure that the Court caters to its policy 
preferences.\20\ The Court would become an extension of the 
political branches rather than an independent tribunal 
defending the Constitution.\21\

  Historical Attempts to Pack the Supreme Court Have Failed and Been 
  Resoundingly Condemned as an Inappropriate Attack on the Institution
    While Article III establishes ``one supreme Court,'' it 
neither fixes the size of the Court nor specifically empowers 
Congress to do so.\33\ Through the Constitution's Necessary and 
Proper Clause, Congress has historically set the number of 
justices on the Supreme Court.\34\ While this number fluctuated 
throughout the 19th Century with the number of federal circuit 
courts, the size of the Supreme Court has remained at nine 
justices since 1869.\35\
---------------------------------------------------------------------------
    \33\U.S. Const. art. III, Sec. 1.
    \34\Joanna R. Lampe, Cong. Research Serv. No. LSB10562, ``Court 
Packing'': Legislative Control over the Size of the Supreme Court 1-2 
(2020).
    \35\Id. at 2-3. During the period when the Court's size fluctuated 
with the number of circuits, justices were responsible for ``riding 
circuit'' and hearing cases at lower levels of the federal courts. Once 
this practice ended, there was no longer a reason to expand or shrink 
the court as new lower courts were created.
---------------------------------------------------------------------------
    The most prominent modern effort to change the size of the 
Supreme Court was President Franklin D. Roosevelt's 1937 
attempt to pack the Court. On February 5, 1937, in the wake of 
his landslide victory in the 1936 presidential election, 
President Roosevelt sent Congress a sweeping judicial reform 
proposal that included an expansion of the Supreme Court.\36\ 
President Roosevelt sought to add an additional justice to the 
Court for every sitting justice who reached the age of 70 years 
and six months, which would have expanded the Court to fifteen 
justices.\37\ This scheme sought to cement a dependable left-
wing majority on the Court after it had repeatedly struck down 
his New Deal legislation as unconstitutional.\38\ In his thinly 
veiled letter to Congress, President Roosevelt stated that 
``lowered mental or physical vigor leads men to avoid an 
examination of complicated and changed conditions. Little by 
little, new facts become blurred through old glasses[.]''\39\

    This court-packing attempt faced opposition from both 
Congress and the Supreme Court itself. Between March and August 
1937, Members of Congress introduced at least ten 
constitutional amendments fixing the number of justices at 
nine.\40\ In hearings before the Senate Judiciary Committee, 
seventy witnesses testified in opposition to the President's 
court-packing plan.\41\ Most witnesses denounced it as an 
attack on judicial independence and separation of powers.\42\ 
Many witnesses shared President Roosevelt's concerns with the 
Court's recent decisions, but believed that court packing was 
the wrong way to address these concerns, stating that it 
violated the spirit of the Constitution, if not the letter.\43\ 
Even then-Chief Justice Charles Evans Hughes entered the fray: 
during the Senate Judiciary hearings, he wrote a letter 
refuting the President's claims about the Court's inefficiency 
and argued that an expansion of the Court would hamper its 
operation.\44\

Committee recommended the President's bill be voted down, 
describing it as ``an invasion of judicial power such as has 
never before been attempted in this country.''\45\ The 
Committee called President Roosevelt's court-packing proposal 
``a measure which should be so emphatically rejected that its 
parallel will never again be presented to the free

attempt to snatch away the judicial power through court 
packing. The principles that animated this opposition--the 
principles that the Senate Judiciary Committee described in 
1937--remain true today.

                        House Joint Resolution 1
    H.J. Res. 1 proposes an amendment to the Constitution to 
set the number of justices at nine.\51\ It would fix the 
current composition of the Court, with one chief justice and 
eight associate justices.\52\ By fixing the number of justices 
and composition of the Court in the Constitution, this 
amendment would prevent the fleeting, partisan whims of 
Congress from undermining the legitimacy of the Court.

                            Dissenting Views
    The question H.J. Res. 1 presents is not really about 
whether nine should suddenly become a magic number of justices 
for the Supreme Court. It's about whether this Republican-
controlled Congress--which has already surrendered 
congressional war powers, congressional taxing and tariff 
powers, and congressional spending powers to a president 
desiring to be a king--should now permanently relinquish 
another constitutional power that the Framers gave to us in 
Article I.
    The Framers understood that the more a president seeks to 
expand the powers of the executive branch, the more important 
it would be for the other branches of government, Congress and 
the Judiciary, to exercise our own powers to serve as a 
structural check on an imperious and rapacious executive 
branch. That's why we have the power of impeachment, the power 
of oversight, the power of the purse. Alas, over the last year 
and a half, we've seen this Republican-controlled Congress act 
in an utterly obsequious and invertebrate fashion before a 
President who thinks he can go to war without Congress, impose 
tariffs without Congress, create billion-dollar slush funds 
without Congress, give himself and his family permanent 
immunity for crimes and civil wrongs committed without Congress 
and reduce the Supreme Court and the federal judiciary to the 
pure instrument of his will.
    Placing loyalty to party over loyalty to the Constitution, 
MAGA House Republicans have repeatedly declined to act in any 
way to reject the executive branch's outrageous usurpations. In 
fairness to the Senate Republicans, they have properly reacted 
with outrage and scorn for the President's attempts to deal 
himself $1.776 billion to bankroll his private militia and 
ship-of-fools political operation. But this joint resolution 
from House Republicans takes us further down the road of 
institutional capitulation and surrender.
    This is not constitutional conservatism but rather one more 
legislative abdication that strikes at the very architecture of 
our republic. The Founding Fathers designed Congress to be the 
``predominant'' branch, the one representing the people 
directly and the one most capable of standing as a bulwark 
against a president grasping for Kingly powers. As the people's 
Representatives, we are meant to protect the Constitution, not 
surrender its authority whenever a president likes his 
particular stacking and packing of power on the Supreme Court 
and wants to freeze it into place. But that's what the 
Administration now demands, and our Republican colleagues fall 
into line like good little foot soldiers, proactively and 
permanently ceding another one of Congress's powers to 
presidential will, for no good reason.
    Over the course of our nation's history, Congress has 
repeatedly exercised its authority to change the size, 
structure, jurisdiction and other aspects of the Court and 
federal judiciary, and for a variety of reasons. The Judiciary 
Act of 1789 established the first Supreme Court with six 
justices--I guess that would have been unlawful and not to the 
liking of our friends across the aisle. Then in 1801, President 
John Adams and Congress shrank the size of the Court from six 
to five, to limit the incoming President Thomas Jefferson's 
ability to make appointments--I guess that would have been 
unconstitutional in the view of our colleagues across the 
aisle, too. When President Jefferson and his Republican 
Majority came into power, Congress restored the sixth seat.
    In 1807, Congress expanded the size of the court to seven 
seats when it created the 7th Circuit Court of Appeals, and 
that became the pattern: there should be one justice for each 
circuit. Again, in 1837, Congress expanded the number of 
federal circuits to nine and added the corresponding additional 
two seats to the Supreme Court, bringing the total number to 
nine. In 1863, Congress briefly added a 10th Circuit during the 
Civil War, and of course, added a 10th justice to the Supreme 
Court, giving President Abraham Lincoln another justice to 
appoint.
    In 1866, Congress abolished the 10th Circuit and then 
reduced the size of the Supreme Court to seven justices. 
Congress did so to deny President Andrew Johnson the ability to 
nominate justices who might undermine Reconstruction. Again, in 
1869, President Ulysses Grant increased the Court to nine 
justices with another Judiciary Act, which set the number at 
nine, matching the number of justices for the number of federal 
circuits.
    So why would we propose a constitutional amendment now, 
after this history, to tie our own hands--and the hands of 
every future Congress--for all time?
    This Resolution is obviously meant to cement into place a 
profoundly partisan and lopsided Supreme Court that was 
successfully stacked and packed by former Senate Majority 
Leader Mitch McConnell after the deaths of Justice Scalia and 
Justice Ginsburg. In the former case, when President Barack 
Obama nominated Merrick Garland to fill the seat, Sen. 
McConnell did not even allow hearings, much less a vote--a full 
11 months before the next election because he said the people 
should decide. Of course, the people had decided, by electing 
President Obama to serve as president for a four-year term. In 
the latter case, Sen. McConnell rushed Amy Coney Barrett's 
nomination to a vote less than two weeks before the 
presidential election.
    This measure before us today is not actually a serious 
effort to improve our constitutional system. It is an attempt
