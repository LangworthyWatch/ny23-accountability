Hochul, Blakeman spar in single debate in race for NY governor
Alex Gault, Watertown Daily Times, N.Y.
Thu, October 15, 2026 at 3:59 AM UTC
11 min read
Add Yahoo as a preferred source to see more of our stories on Google.
The two candidates for governor of New York met for their first and only debate on Tuesday night, sparring over their visions for the future of the state and seeking to show voters the gaps in their opponent's plans and policies.
Incumbent Democratic Gov. Kathleen C. Hochul faced Nassau County executive and GOP candidate Bruce R. Blakeman for the hour-long forum hosted by Spectrum News in New York City. Both candidates came in with their points prepared; Hochul was ready with barbs about what she classified as "lies" from Blakeman, while the GOP candidate had a list of areas where he said the incumbent has failed New Yorkers.
Hochul sought to tie Blakeman to President Donald J. Trump, who is among the most unpopular figures in New York right now, while Blakeman mostly stuck to Hochul's record on its own.

At times, both candidates went back and forth over the objections of the debate's hosts, Spectrum New York 1's Errol T. Louis and Spectrum 'Capitol Tonight' anchor Susan Arbetter.
Blakeman opened with a salvo against Hochul's tax policies, pointing out that New York is the highest taxed state in the country and has seen a precipitous rise in utility rates that have squeezed New Yorker's budgets.
"As governor, I will cut taxes, I will cut utility bills in half and I will create safer communities, just like I did in my own community in Nassau County," Blakeman said.
He also hammered Hochul for allegedly spending $8 billion in taxpayer dollars given to undocumented immigrants for supportive housing, food, healthcare and cell phones. But the state's own records indicate that only $3.34 billion in state funding has gone to those programs, most of it aimed at addressing the influx of undocumented immigrants bused into the state by southern state governors during the Biden administration.

"I'm going to ask you to vote for change," he said, addressing the voters directly down the camera. "What have you got to lose?"
Hochul opened her own message with a biography about herself; born to a family living in a trailer park, she said she worked in a restaurant to get the money to go to college.
"I never forgot where I came from, the struggles, and that's why as governor I've been laser-focused on affordability and your family's safety, and that's why we delivered the largest middle-class (tax) rate cut in 70 years," she said.
She pointed to the $10 billion in combined "rebates" she's passed through the five state budgets she's overseen as governor; tax money sent back to the taxpayers that she's said are refunds for higher-than-expected sales tax income from inflation, a refund to help cover high energy costs and tax credits for families with young children.

Advertisement
She boosted her work on addressing crime and public safety; after taking office at the height of a pandemic-fueled spike in property and violent crimes, Hochul has overseen a precipitous drop in crime rates across most regions of the state and seen New York hit the lowest crime rates in modern history.
Repeatedly Hochul sought to link Blakeman to Trump; she said the GOP candidate, endorsed by the president, wants to bring "Trump-like leadership to Albany," and later in the debate hammered him over his enthusiastic presence at a Trump campaign rally in Syracuse last week.
Blakeman took the chance to distance himself from the president; while he has thus-far declined to comment on differences between his own politics and that of the president, Blakeman on Tuesday night said they differ on the issue of abortion.
"I have very significant policy differences with President Trump; he's pro-life, I'm pro-choice. I believe in a guest worker program for immigrants, he's isn't there yet," Blakeman said to retort one of Hochul's statements, as host Arbetter tried to move the debate along.

Advertisement
Despite spending much of his campaign seeking to tie Hochul to New York City's democratic socialist mayor Zohran K. Mamdani, Blakeman largely declined to bring the mayor up on Tuesday, only first referring to him about 45 minutes into the debate during another back-and-forth over Blakeman's affinity for Trump.
On the issues, Blakeman and Hochul painted vastly different visions of New York under their administrations. On coordination with federal immigration authorities, Blakeman said he wholeheartedly backs coordination between local police agencies and federal agencies. He blamed Hochul for enacting policies that protect undocumented immigrants, whom he accused of committing crimes.
"What do you say to all those people who've had their children hooked on drugs by illegal immigrants?" he said.
Hochul pushed for a ban to keep local police from coordinating with federal immigration authorities earlier this year, and she criticized Blakeman for backing immigration authorities while they stand accused of overly aggressive assaults on immigrants and citizens alike. She invoked the recent incident where Immigration and Customs Enforcement agents shot live rounds into a vehicle in the Marble Hill neighborhood of Manhattan with a young child inside.
Advertisement
Advertisement
"He basically said in the state of New York he'd be fine if ICE agents shoot into cars, shoot guns into vehicles not knowing who is inside," Hochul said.
On energy policies, Hochul said she backed this year's utility rebate tax credit that's giving a few hundred dollars to most New Yorkers as a short-term solution to a long-term problem where energy demand is increasing while access to supply is waning. She said she's pushing for an "all-of-the-above" energy approach that relies on existing fossil fuel and some new natural gas generation alongside renewables and nuclear energy, but blamed the Trump administration for blocking major projects.
Notably, Hochul also accused the Trump White House of standing in her way on new nuclear power generation plans; she's pushing for the state to build a 1-gigawatt nuclear plant with private assistance, and separately trying to encourage another 4 gigawatts of privately-funded nuclear power development as well. Since those projects started last year, Hochul has regularly pitched them as a point of collaboration between her administration and the president, but it appears that collaboration has not progressed smoothly.
"As part of my all-of-the-above I'm trying to do nuclear, but the White House, playing politics, won't sign off on it," she said.

Advertisement
According to an administration spokesperson reached after the debate, Hochul was referring to the American Nuclear Supply Chain loan program, being run by the U.S. Department of Energy, which aims to support efforts to procure expensive components for a specific type of nuclear plant, the Westinghouse AP1000 that is currently the only licensed design allowed for construction in the U.S. The Trump administration has not moved those loans forward despite a pledge to fast-track them.
She also blamed Trump for starting a war with Iran that has choked off a major shipping lane for oil products, driving the cost of gasoline and diesel fuel to record highs across the globe.
Blakeman, for his part, defended the president and blamed Hochul for the repeated utility rate hikes that her administration, through the Public Service Commission, has authorized year after year for every utility provider in the state.
He said under his watch he would allow hydrofracking to collect natural gas from the Marcellus Shale underneath the Southern Tier, and he repeated a claim that he's made numerous times throughout the campaign.
Advertisement
Advertisement
"On the Pennsylvania side of the border people are driving around in brand new Cadillac Escalades and on the New York side of the border they're driving around in 25-year-old rusted out pickup trucks," he said.
Evidence doesn't support his argument that hydrofracking would boost the regional economy in the Southern Tier. A 2023 analysis of the effects of hydrofracking in northern Pennsylvania, which accesses the Marcellus and Utica natural gas shales, by the Marcellus Coalition found that the impact on local economies that host fracking operations is less than anticipated, largely driving revenues in the larger cities and for companies not based in Pennsylvania. Blakeman claims that natural gas extraction in Pennsylvania has driven their utility costs down; New Yorkers pay about 50% more in utility fees than Pennsylvanians.
Blakeman has repeatedly claimed he would cut utility bills "in half" on his first day as governor, but has not put forward a detailed plan to achieve that lofty goal. When pressed by host Arbetter, he said said that he would address taxes and delivery fees, which he said are the main drivers of increasing utility bills. He also pointed to the state's Clean Energy Fund, which skims some dollars from utility bills to invest in clean energy development projects.
"Her slush fund, where she collects billions of dollars from you and invests them in science projects like wind turbines in the middle of the ocean or solar farms in upstate New York that are taking up our rich farmland, which by the way are covered by snow three months out of the year," he said.

Advertisement
Later in the debate, during a rapid-fire questioning round, Blakeman said he would support reopening the Indian Point nuclear power plant in White Plains, Westchester County. That facility closed in 2019 under the leadership of then-Gov. Andrew M. Cuomo, who bowed to environmentalists who expressed fears the facility was hurting fish stocks in the Hudson River and posed a threat to New York City if it ever melted down.
Hochul hasn't said much about Indian Point, and when she was asked about it Tuesday night pivoted to discuss her push to open new nuclear power plants further upstate.
The two candidates also sparred over their plans for large-scale data centers, an issue closely tied with the environment and energy prices. Hochul has signed a one-year moratorium into effect and tasked her executive agencies to study the issue and work up a plan for regulations on future developments. Blakeman, aligned with Trump on the issue, has disagreed with the moratorium and argued it has taken decision-making out of local hands.
Despite the acrimony in their words, the debate ended cordially with Hochul and Blakeman shaking hands. They appeared to share a few brief words that weren't aired before Hochul stepped away and the program moved on.

Advertisement
Both candidates ended the debate claiming victory. Hochul's campaign team had been sending reporters fact-checks of Blakeman's claims live throughout the debate, and in a statement after the debate Hochul's team accused her opponent of failing to stick to the truth.
""Tonight on live TV, New York's toughest mom was all offense, and Trump's puppet was just…off," said Sarafina Chitika, spokesperson for the Hochul campaign. "While Blakeman straight up lied about his promise — caught on tape — to cut Medicaid 'in half,' and looked visibly shaken when confronted with his record of hiking property taxes, Governor Hochul proved again that she's relentlessly-focused on lowering costs and keeping families safe."
In his own statement, Blakeman claimed he was the one on offense during the debate and said he'd held the governor to account for her policies.
"Kathy Hochul has spent five years making New York unaffordable and unsafe, and tonight she couldn't defend a minute of it," he said. "Her policies have left New Yorkers paying the highest taxes in the nation and utility bills 70% above the national average, costing families thousands more every year. Meanwhile, as hardworking New Yorkers struggle to keep the lights on, Hochul has spent billions of their taxpayer dollars on luxury hotel rooms, free healthcare, free food, and free cell phones for illegal migrants—all while refusing to apologize for her sanctuary city policies and dangerous loopholes that have allowed violent criminals to roam free, resulting in horrific migrant rapes and murders across our state."
Blakeman followed that up with a fundraising appeal shortly afterward, again arguing he had won the debate and asking donors to "chip in $25 right now to help finish the job and defeat the 'Worst Governor in America', Kathy Hochul".
The full debate can be seen online on Spectrum's Youtube page,  tinyurl.com/DebateNYGov .
Voting has already started in this year's elections; early mail-in and absentee ballots started going out earlier last month. Early in-person voting will open on Oct. 24, and election day is Nov. 3.
