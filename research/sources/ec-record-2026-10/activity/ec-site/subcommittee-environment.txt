Subcommittee
 on 
Environment
All matters related to soil, air, noise and water contamination; emergency environmental response, both physical and cybersecurity. In particular, the subcommittee has jurisdiction over The Nuclear Waste Policy Act, The Clean Air Act, The Safe Drinking Water Act, Comprehensive Environmental Response Compensation and Liability Act – including Superfund and the Emergency Planning and Community Right to Know Act, The Solid Waste Disposal Act, The Toxic Substance Control Act and The Chemical Facility Anti-Terrorism Standards Program. Under the Clean Air Act, this subcommittee deals with National Ambient Air Quality Standards (NAAQS) for criteria pollutants; National Emissions Standards for Hazardous Air Pollutants (NESHAP) Standards; New Source Performance Standards (NSPS); Mobile Source Standards for vehicles, aircraft, fuels and fuel additives, including the Renewable Fuel Standard (RFS) and greenhouse gas emissions from motor vehicles. The subcommittee focuses on the regulation of solid, hazardous, and nuclear wastes, including mining, nuclear, oil, gas, and coal combustion waste.

House Passes Resolutions to Overturn Biden-Harris EPA’s Costly California Maritime Waivers
WASHINGTON, D.C.
 – Today, Congressman Brett Guthrie (KY-02), Chairman of the House Committee on Energy and Commerce, and Congressman Gary Palmer (AL-06), Chairman of the Subcommittee on the Environment, applauded the House passage of H.J. Res 210 and H.J. Res. 213 to overturn the Biden-Harris Administration’s EPA waivers that harmed the ability of ocean-going vessels and commercial harbor craft to operate in California ports.
“The Biden-Harris Administration’s EPA approved waivers allowing California to impose costly and burdensome emissions mandates—without meaningful reductions in emissions—on ocean-going tankers docked in California waters and on the ferries, tugboats, work boats, and commercial fishing boats our national economy relies on due to the importance of West Coast ports to interstate commerce. These rules are both impracticable and expensive; ultimately, they fail to achieve their emissions goals while putting new burdens on American businesses,”
said Chairman Guthrie.
“These waivers have a national impact, and it is important for Congress to use oversight tools like the Congressional Review Act to protect American consumers. Throughout this Congress, our Committee has continued to stand up to California’s far-left environmental overreach, and H.J. Res. 210 and H.J. Res. 213 continue that important work. Thank you to Congressman Fong and Congressman Gallagher for your leadership to overturn these unworkable California waivers.”
“California should not be allowed to dictate national environmental policy or impose its radical agenda on families and businesses across the country,”
said Chairman Palmer
. 
“As Chairman of the Environment Subcommittee, I am proud of our continued work to rein in Biden-era regulatory overreach. Passage of these resolutions will protect critical supply chains and prevent costly, impractical mandates from being passed on to American consumers.”
BACKGROUND:
Clean Air Act section 209 generally preempts states from regulating emissions from motor vehicles and other mobile sources, unless EPA grants California a waiver of preemption based on a determination that the state standards are at least as stringent as federal standards and are needed by the state “to meet compelling and extraordinary conditions.” The Clean Air Act allows other states to adopt California standards.
H.J. Res. 210:
The resolution overturns the Biden-Harris EPA’s approval of a Clean Air Act waiver allowing California to regulate emissions from ocean-going tanker ships while docked at marine terminals in California.
California regulated emissions from ocean-going container, passenger, and refrigerated cargo vessels in 2010, and the Obama EPA granted a waiver of preemption allowing those regulations to go into effect in 2011. In 2020, California amended its regulations to also cover tanker ships and roll-on/roll-off ships. EPA approved a new waiver in 2023.
These state regulations are disrupting oil imports to the state and further contributing to closures of refineries, including ones that supply critical jet fuel for military use and commercial transportation.
The technology needed to comply with state regulations does not reliably and affordably exist yet for oil tankers, making the rules impracticable and compliance virtually impossible.
H.J. Res. 213:
The resolution overturns the Biden-Harris EPA’s 2025 approval of California emissions regulations for commercial harbor craft, including ferries, tugboats, work boats, and commercial fishing boats.
The California regulations at issue require all ferries and new excursion vessels to install zero-emission and advanced technologies. For other categories of commercial harbor craft, both existing and new, the California rules would require compliance with the most stringent federal marine emission standards and use of control technologies such as diesel particulate filters that are not widely available, making compliance exceptionally difficult.
The California rules are impracticable, costly, and likely to cause unintended safety risks. Industry estimates expect the rules to impose more than $2.5 billion in costs to maritime operators and consumers without a meaningful reduction in emissions.

Chairman Palmer Delivers Opening Statement at Environment Subcommittee Hearing to Examine Legislation to Safeguard America’s Drinking Water
WASHINGTON, D.C.
 – Congressman Gary Palmer (AL-06), Chairman of the Subcommittee on Environment, delivered the following opening statement at today’s hearing titled 
Reliable Water in the Digital Age: Examining Legislation to Strengthen Drinking Water Systems and Protect Ratepayers
.
Subcommittee Chairman Palmer’s opening statement as prepared for delivery:
“Good morning. I want to welcome Subcommittee Ranking Member Tonko, my colleagues, and our witnesses to today’s hearing before the Subcommittee on Environment.
“Today’s hearing provides a timely and important opportunity to examine legislation that would reauthorize critical drinking water programs and address the growing water infrastructure demands associated with data centers.
“Congress enacted the Safe Drinking Water Act in 1974 to protect the quality of our nation’s drinking water. Since then, Congress has periodically amended the Act and reauthorized its programs to address emerging needs and challenges. Most notably, the 1996 amendments established the Drinking Water State Revolving Fund, or DWSRF, which has become the primary source of federal investment in drinking water infrastructure.
“One of the discussion drafts we will consider today – the Safe Drinking Water Infrastructure Improvement Act of 2026 – would reauthorize the DWSRF and includes a number of bipartisan priorities, including support for rural and smaller water systems, a program to provide point-of-use filters for drinking water systems at schools, and new authority to address cybersecurity risks.
“We will continue to discuss these policy reforms and appropriate funding authorizations with our friends in the minority, as we also work with our colleagues across the House and in the Senate on a final water infrastructure bill.
“In addition to reauthorizing these programs, we will examine legislation intended to protect ratepayers from costs associated with serving data centers. Data centers can and should have positive impacts on local communities. They are major drivers of investment, economic growth, job creation, and the development of our nation’s digital infrastructure. However, those benefits should not come at the expense of families and small businesses through higher water rates.
“In my district alone, QTS has announced a $15 billion investment in data center development, creating significant opportunities for economic growth, job creation, and digital infrastructure development across Alabama. We welcome that investment. At the same time, local families and businesses should not be forced to shoulder infrastructure costs that are directly attributable to these facilities.
“Many major tech companies have also committed to reducing or replenishing their water use. Companies are investing in closed-loop systems, liquid cooling, and other technologies intended to reduce their reliance on evaporative cooling. We should recognize and encourage these innovations while continuing to ensure that communities and ratepayers are protected.
“The two pieces of draft legislation on today’s agenda -- the Safe Drinking Water Infrastructure Improvement Act of 2026 and the Water Cost Accountability Act -- would together support continued economic growth by strengthening our nation’s drinking water infrastructure.
“To assist us in these efforts, we welcome three witnesses today.
“First, we have Mr. Neil Bradley, Executive Vice President, Chief Policy Officer, and Head of Strategic Advocacy at the U.S. Chamber of Commerce.
“We are also joined by Ms. Mae Wu, founder and principal of Health and Environmental Advising.
“Finally, we will hear from Ms. Julie Moore, Secretary of the Vermont Agency of Natural Resources and vice chair of the Water Committee for the Environmental Council of the States, or ECOS.
“I thank each of our witnesses for appearing before the Subcommittee today, and I look forward to our discussion.”

Environment Subcommittee Holds Hearing to Safeguard America’s Drinking Water
WASHINGTON, D.C.
 – Today, Congressman Gary Palmer (AL-06), Chairman of the Subcommittee on Environment, led a hearing titled 
Reliable Water in the Digital Age: Examining Legislation to Strengthen Drinking Water Systems and Protect Ratepayers.
“Data centers can and should have positive impacts on local communities,”
said Chairman Palmer.
“They are major drivers of investment, economic growth, job creation, and the development of our nation’s digital infrastructure. However, those benefits should not come at the expense of families and small businesses through higher water rates.”
Watch the full hearing 
here
.
Below are key excerpts from today’s hearing:
Congressman Dan Crenshaw (TX-02):
“There's a good bill today that seeks to protect ratepayers as well as water quality and supply — especially in the modern age, where an increasing number of data centers are being built. There are trade-offs worth raising: concerns about water supply, grid strain, and electricity prices. But these are solvable problems, and today's bills are aimed at solving them.”
Congressman John Joyce, M.D. (PA-13):
“This risk is especially urgent in rural communities with smaller water systems. In meetings with many of the municipalities I represent, I have heard regularly from small water and sewer authorities struggling to maintain infrastructure nearing the end of its life while they wait for the funding and resources needed for replacements and upgrades. A disruption in either service strains both systems and places serious burdens on the community and on the operators who maintain that infrastructure.
“In the draft legislation we are considering today, I'm glad to see a commitment to maintaining and modernizing the Drinking Water State Revolving Fund — a necessary federal funding source that communities in my district rely on. In preparation for this hearing, I heard from the manager of the Altoona Water Authority in my district about how these funds have allowed them to complete necessary projects, from waterline replacements to treatment facility improvements, all while maintaining low customer rates and delivering the cleanest water to the highest standards.”
Congressman Buddy Carter (GA-01):
“Look, data centers are a concern — but I believe data centers can be very good for our communities. They can bring major private investment, create jobs, strengthen the tax base, and open new opportunities for local workers and businesses. But the projects that are going to be successful over the long term are the ones that operate as partners with the communities that host them.
“I know this firsthand. In another life, I was a mayor, and I recognize how important local jurisdiction is. So I would encourage the data centers — not after the fact, but before the fact — to please work with the local communities. This will help us all.”
