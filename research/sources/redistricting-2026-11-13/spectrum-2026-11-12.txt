(AP Photo/Hans Pennink) Hochul weighs New York redistricting push after Democrats’ election gains BY Nái Rhoden Buffalo PUBLISHED 7:30 PM ET Nov. 12, 2026 PUBLISHED 7:30 PM EST Nov. 12, 2026 SHARE ALBANY, N.Y. — New York Democrats pushed to redraw the state’s congressional maps to counter Republican redistricting efforts elsewhere in the country. But after Democrats made substantial gains in last week’s elections, Gov. Kathy Hochul is reconsidering the next move. The proposed constitutional amendment would allow New York to redraw congressional districts before 2030 and give state lawmakers greater control over the process. It would also remove existing constitutional restrictions against drawing districts to favor a political party or incumbent. Lawmakers approved the amendment for the first time in June. It must pass the Legislature again before going to voters for approval. Hochul has not committed to moving forward. “The political landscape has changed,” Hochul said, adding that she plans to consult House Democratic leader Hakeem Jeffries and New York’s legislative leaders before deciding what comes next. The governor also pointed out that lawmakers have already completed the first step. “We’re halfway there. We already passed one session,” she said. Republicans question the proposal Former Republican Rep. John Faso argues that the amendment would undermine redistricting protections New Yorkers approved in 2014. Faso said the proposal would allow lawmakers to disregard county, town and other municipal boundaries when drawing congressional districts. He also accused Democrats of seeking a political advantage by changing the rules. But Republicans have pursued mid-decade redistricting in other states, including Texas and North Carolina. Asked why Democrats in New York should be held to a different standard, Faso said he opposed those Republican efforts as well. “As my mother used to tell me, two wrongs don’t make a right,” Faso said. “And I’m very much against midterm redistricting.” Faso argued that both parties should focus on winning elections rather than redrawing districts for political advantage. Election results change the calculation Jeffrey Wice, a redistricting expert and professor at New York Law School, said Democrats now face a different political calculation than they did when lawmakers first approved the amendment. He pointed to Republican redistricting efforts in Florida, North Carolina and Texas that failed to produce the electoral gains Republicans had sought. “They nearly lost every district they tried to pick up,” Wice said. Wice said Democrats must now consider whether another redistricting battle is worth the financial and political cost, particularly after their stronger-than-expected congressional gains. He also cautioned that the political landscape could change again before the next election. What happens next? New York lawmakers could take up the constitutional amendment again in 2027. If it passes a second time, voters could decide whether to approve it as soon as November 2027. Approval would open the door to new congressional maps for the 2028 elections, although lawmakers would still have to decide whether to redraw them. For now, the proposal remains on the table as Hochul and Democratic legislative leaders weigh whether the election results have changed the need for another redistricting fight.
