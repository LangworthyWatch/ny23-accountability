# Fact check brief: Langworthy CHECK Act transparency post

## Purpose

Register the August 11, 2026 post as a Ledger record. The angle is not that the
post contains false statements. It largely does not. The angle is the gap
between what the bill does and what the post implies it does, set against the
member's voting record on health care affordability.

Discipline note for drafting: this piece fails if it overclaims. Do not assert
that the CHECK Act is worthless or that every cost increase traces to one vote.
The contrast is strong enough without stretching.

## Claim register

### C1. Langworthy introduced the CHECK Act

- Verdict (preliminary): TRUE
- Evidence: his office announced introduction of the Clear Healthcare Expense
  Cost Knowledge (CHECK) Act on June 3, 2026. Coverage in the Observer, WIVB,
  and Chautauqua Today the same week. He was quoted on the bill at a House
  Energy and Commerce health subcommittee hearing on price transparency.
- Verification needed: bill number, text, cosponsor list, current status.

### C2. The bill provides transparency, exposes hidden costs, introduces accountability

- Verdict (preliminary): SUBSTANTIALLY TRUE AS TO CONTENT
- Evidence per his office and contemporaneous coverage: strengthens oversight of
  pharmacy benefit managers, third party administrators, and other middlemen;
  requires itemized bills before providers may seek payment or pursue
  collections; protects patients from charges exceeding previously disclosed
  estimates absent unforeseen medical necessity; prohibits gag clauses; requires
  timely explanations of benefits; gives employers more visibility into
  spending; includes compliance penalties.
- Fair treatment note: PBM oversight and gag clause prohibitions are real,
  bipartisan, and meaningful policy. NY-23 has lost independent pharmacies, and
  PBM reform speaks to that directly. The Ledger should credit this plainly.
- Verification needed: read the bill text. Confirm the penalty structure has
  teeth and confirm what is genuinely new versus what already exists under the
  2021 hospital price transparency rule, the No Surprises Act, and the Lower
  Costs, More Transparency Act framework.

### C3. The days of surprise medical bills must end

- Verdict (preliminary): MISLEADING BY OMISSION
- The No Surprises Act took effect January 1, 2022 and already bans balance
  billing for most emergency care and for out of network care at in network
  facilities. The post presents surprise billing as an unaddressed frontier.
- Verification needed: confirm what categories of surprise billing the CHECK Act
  reaches that the No Surprises Act does not, particularly ground ambulance,
  which remains a known gap. If the CHECK Act closes the ground ambulance gap,
  that is a genuine credit and should be stated.

### C4. The implied claim: transparency equals affordability

- Verdict (preliminary): UNSUPPORTED
- The post's grocery and gasoline analogy assumes a shoppable market. Most
  health spending is not shoppable. Emergency care, inpatient admissions, and
  urgent diagnoses do not involve price comparison. Disclosure tells a patient
  what a bill will be. It does not change what the bill is.
- Verification needed: pull evaluation literature on the 2021 hospital price
  transparency rule and its measured effect on prices and out of pocket
  spending. This is the empirical spine of the piece and must be sourced to peer
  reviewed or nonpartisan analysis, not advocacy groups.

### C5. The contrast: his votes on health care spending

- Verdict (preliminary): SUPPORTED, pending roll call retrieval
- H.R. 1, the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, passed the House 218 to 214 on
  July 3, 2025. The only Republicans voting no were Thomas Massie and Brian
  Fitzpatrick. Langworthy gave floor remarks in support and sits on the Rules
  Committee, where he voted against Democratic amendments to the rule, including
  a motion tied to health care.
- Contents relevant to affordability: CBO scored the law as reducing federal
  health spending by roughly $1 trillion over a decade through Medicaid
  eligibility and work reporting requirements, provider tax restrictions, cost
  sharing changes, and restrictions on lawfully present immigrants. The law did
  not extend the enhanced ACA premium tax credits, which lapsed at the end of
  2025, resetting subsidy eligibility to pre 2021 rules and raising out of
  pocket premiums for marketplace enrollees.
- On the subsidies specifically, LangworthyWatch already has a November 2025
  record on his refusal to commit to an extension vote. Cross link it.
- Verification needed: pull Roll Call 190 from clerk.house.gov and cite his name
  and vote directly. Do not rely on the inference from the two Republican no
  votes, even though the inference is sound.

## Handling the causal claim carefully

Do not write that health care costs are rising because of a war. There is no
sourced basis for that and it will be the first thing an opponent attacks.

Do not write that people have already lost coverage because of H.R. 1 in
specific numbers. FactCheck.org published a piece in July 2026 pushing back on
Democratic claims that the law had already driven measurable ACA and Medicaid
declines. Overstating timing is the most likely way this piece gets discredited.

The defensible causal statement is narrower and stronger:

> The enhanced premium tax credits expired at the end of 2025 without extension.
> For a marketplace enrollee in NY-23, that is a direct, documented increase in
> what they pay. Langworthy voted for the law that let them lapse and has not
> committed to a vote restoring them. The CHECK Act would tell that same person
> what their care costs. It would not change what they pay for it.

## Editorial posture

He is not wrong that health care pricing is opaque, and PBM oversight is real
policy that his district needs. The piece should say so early and without
hedging, because the credibility of everything after it depends on that.

The problem is the frame. A price tag is not a price cut. The post asks voters
to measure him by a bill that discloses costs while the vote that moved costs
went the other way. Transparency is the cheapest possible answer to
affordability, because it costs the federal government nothing and asks nothing
of anyone who profits from the current prices.
