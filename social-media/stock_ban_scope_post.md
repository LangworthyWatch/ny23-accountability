The stock trading ban he just passed does not cover the President, the Vice President, or a single Cabinet official. Those words appear zero times in the bill.

CLAIM: "Americans from across the political spectrum overwhelmingly support banning members of Congress from trading stocks... It passed the House and now it's time for the Senate to act."
VERDICT: Misleading

First, what is accurate, and we will say it plainly:
→ It did pass the House, 232-198 on July 22 (Roll Call 280). He voted yes.
→ The 86% is real, and so are the splits: R 87%, D 88%, I 81%.

What the post leaves out:

→ The poll printed on his own graphic asks about prohibiting Members from TRADING individual stocks. The bill bans buying them and permits selling them, with 7 to 14 days of advance public notice. Those are two different policies, and the 86% measures the broader one.

→ That notice requirement is a real constraint and we are not calling it meaningless. A member cannot quietly dump a position the afternoon of a closed briefing. It is still not a prohibition.

→ The bill's covered persons are Members of Congress, spouses, and dependent children. That is the whole list. A Cabinet secretary could buy and sell individual stocks the day it became law and nothing in it would reach them.

A local footnote, offered as context and not as a strike against the bill: Western New York produced the most prominent congressional insider trading case in the country. Rep. Chris Collins tipped his adult son, who tipped his fiancée's father. Neither would be a covered person here. That is not a flaw in the bill. What Collins did was already illegal, he was prosecuted and convicted, and no trading ban can reach an adult child's own money. He was pardoned in December 2020.

One more thing already on the record: at the Rules Committee, the bill's own sponsor was asked whether members can still sell as much stock as they like under it. He answered that the description was "accurate."

Don't take our word for it. Read the bill text and the roll call.
