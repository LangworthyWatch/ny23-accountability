Canada's worst fire season on record was the quietest U.S. year in the same five-season window.

Acres burned, side by side:

→ 2022: Canada 4.4M, U.S. 7.6M
→ 2023: Canada 45.7M, U.S. 2.7M
→ 2024: Canada 13.3M, U.S. 8.9M
→ 2025: Canada 21.7M, U.S. 5.1M
→ 2026 so far: Canada 7.4M, U.S. 3.9M

Over four complete seasons Canada burned 85.2 million acres to America's 24.3 million, about 3.5 times as much.

So why the gap? Mostly not forest management.

→ About 40% of Canada's forest sits in zones where fires are monitored rather than fought, by design, because they are too remote to reach
→ Lightning causes about half of Canada's fires but drove roughly 93% of the area burned in the record 2023 season. Those ignitions come in clusters, far from any road or water source
→ In the U.S., 85 to 89% of fires are human-ignited. They start near roads and crews, get spotted fast, and get hit hard
→ The boreal forest is a crown-fire system. Black spruce and jack pine need fire to reproduce. Stand-replacing burns are the baseline there, not a failure

What is genuinely getting worse is the climate signal. Canada has warmed roughly twice the global rate since 1948, and about three times in the North. Its 10-year average area burned rose 36% between the 2005-2014 and 2015-2024 windows.

One thing the raw acreage hides: a large share of U.S. acres burned is grass and rangeland, not forest. The forest gap is wider than these numbers show.
