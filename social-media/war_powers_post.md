The House has now voted twice to end the Iran war. Nick Langworthy voted no both times, and five more times besides.

One of those votes failed on a 212 to 212 tie. One vote the other way would have carried it.

Then it got stranger. On May 1 the President told Congress the operation was "terminated." Fighting resumed July 6. The strikes since are being called "overseas operations."

Rep. Thomas Massie, a Republican, on what that accomplishes: "The Pentagon is pretending there have been two Iran wars separated by a brief cease-fire." He calls it an absurd ruse, and says going past 90 days without authorization breaks the law.

Here is why it matters beyond the label. The same relabeling that restarts the war powers clock also decides which operation a dead or wounded service member gets counted under. Twelve senators, including Kirsten Gillibrand, are now formally asking the Pentagon why its public casualty database showed 14 dead when 18 had been reported.

→ 5 votes against ending the Iran war, plus 2 on Lebanon. Nay every time.
→ 2 of those resolutions passed the House anyway. One also passed the Senate.
→ No authorization for the use of military force exists for any of it.

We asked what lawyers make of the "terminated" theory. Jack Goldsmith, who ran the Office of Legal Counsel under George W. Bush, wrote: "I find this interpretation of the WPR implausible." Critics at Just Security reached the same word independently. Goldsmith also flags the letter's claim that there had been "no exchange of fire" as highly misleading.

In fairness, and we will say it plainly: presidents of both parties have stretched this statute for fifty years, and no court has ever struck down a concurrent resolution in the war powers context. Langworthy's votes are not unusual for a member of his party. What the record shows is that when his own chamber said stop, twice, he voted no.

Don't take our word for it. Every vote is a roll call you can open yourself.
