U.S. natural gas production went up 4% in 2025. The price went up 61%.

Rep. Langworthy pitches his Energy Choice Act as a way to protect "energy that is affordable, reliable, and proven." But more gas at home doesn't guarantee cheaper gas at home, because a growing share of it is sold on the world market.

Here's what the U.S. Energy Information Administration's own numbers show:

→ Production rose from 103.1 to 107.6 billion cubic feet a day in 2025. The benchmark Henry Hub price rose from $2.19 to $3.53.
→ The U.S. is the world's largest exporter of liquefied natural gas (LNG). Exports rose from 11.9 to 15.1 billion cubic feet a day in 2025, and EIA projects 17.6 this year. That's about 16% of U.S. production, up from about 12% in 2024.
→ In 2022, EIA cited high European demand for U.S. gas as a factor in early-2022 prices. Henry Hub averaged $6.45, the highest since 2008.
→ Later that year, a fire shut the Freeport LNG export terminal. With less gas leaving the country, U.S. prices fell 40% from early June to July 4.
→ Weather still matters: Winter Storm Fern pushed the January 2026 average to $7.72.

When a growing share of our gas is sold overseas, demand abroad can move the price here, even when we drill more.

The fair reading: more supply does help. EIA says rising production keeps storage full, which limits price spikes, and it forecasts about $3.16 for 2027. And one year is a short window; 2025's increase came off a low 2024 price.

The Energy Choice Act itself doesn't touch any of this. It blocks state and local limits on gas hookups and connections. It does nothing about what the gas costs, and it would deepen reliance on a fuel whose price is increasingly tied to world demand.
