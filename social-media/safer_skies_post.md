The attacker was the pilot. His bill screens passengers.

On Sept 30, hours after a Flydubai pilot stabbed his co-pilot at the controls of a Dubai-to-Tel Aviv flight, he posted: "pass my Safer Skies Act to ensure our planes can never again be used as a weapon of terror." We read the bill.

CLAIM: Passing the Safer Skies Act would keep planes from being "used as a weapon of terror" like this one.
VERDICT: MISLEADING

→ H.R. 2353 does one thing: it puts U.S. scheduled public-charter passengers, the ones who sell seats and skip TSA checkpoints, through TSA screening. That is the entire operative text.
→ It has no provision on pilots, crew vetting, cockpit access, flight-deck procedures or foreign carriers.
→ On FZ1073, the person who tried to bring down the plane was one of the two people the airline put in the cockpit. Passengers had to storm the flight deck to stop him. 174 aboard. A 14,125-foot drop in 29 seconds.
→ "Attempted hijacking and terrorist attack": Israel's defense minister said that, "without providing details" (NPR). The airline says the motive is "unknown and remain[s] subject to a formal investigation." The UAE regulator is investigating.
→ The bill's status: referred to a Homeland Security subcommittee March 26, 2025. No hearing, no markup, in 18 months. The House is out until Nov 9.

The fair reading: the charter loophole is real, the bill is evenly bipartisan with 52 cosponsors, and he chairs the Aviation Safety Caucus. Aviation security is his lane. None of that makes this bill the answer to this event.

An aviation security consultant put it to Al Jazeera this way: whatever the motive, "the insider threat is probably the greatest threat to civil aviation nowadays." His bill is about the line you stand in before you board.

Every quote is from the bill text and same-day reporting, cited in the entry.
