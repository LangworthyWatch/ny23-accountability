## Why this matters

A member of the House Energy and Commerce Committee, which writes the
nation's technology law, told his constituents twice in one week that the
public's misgivings about artificial intelligence are foreign propaganda.
If that is true it should be shown, because it would change how every AI
bill he votes on ought to be read. If it is a stretch, then half of the
country has just been told that its own concerns are not its own. This entry checks what the public record
holds.

---

## Statement

**Source:** Official Facebook page. Two clips.

September 23, 2026, from a "Frankly Speaking" interview clip on his
quantum computing bill:

> "We're in a moon race with the Chinese right now. Whoever has AI and
> quantum supremacy will be the world superpower. And a lot of the negative
> information you see out there didn't sprout up by accident. You notice how
> all the negativity that's just popped out of nowhere all over social
> media? There is a Chinese disinformation campaign coming down in this
> country and we don't even know it. It's being funded because they don't
> want America to compete. They want to beat us from within."

September 24, 2026, a reel from his September 21 press conference at a
Clarence Center firehouse:

> "There's a lot of propaganda on this topic that's out there. A lot of it
> being fed by the big superpowers within AI, those companies that are
> already developed, because they don't want competition. So now they want
> regulation to push out any people that might get their piece of the pie.
> There's also a fear factor that I believe is part of a Chinese propaganda
> campaign to try to scare the American people on this."

Neither clip names a source, an agency, a report or a platform.

## The record

### What a Chinese campaign on this subject actually consists of

There is one. It is documented by two private companies, and it is narrow.

On June 10, 2026, OpenAI reported banning "two clusters of ChatGPT
accounts likely originating from China" used "in support of apparent covert
influence operations." The first "generated social media comments and
images claiming that data center buildouts for AI were increasing
electricity prices for average families." OpenAI called it the "Data Center
Bandwagon" campaign. The second criticized U.S. tariffs.

On August 27, 2026, X said it had suspended a suspected Chinese bot
network. Axios: "Roughly 200 accounts from a suspected Chinese bot farm
have quietly tried to influence Americans to oppose AI data centers on
social media." Deseret News, citing X: the wider operation "included around
200,000 inauthentic accounts, with 200 focused on swaying AI infrastructure
debates with warnings of rising energy rates and images of corporations
destroying communities."

| Finding | Who found it | Subject | Scale on AI | Effect claimed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| "Data Center Bandwagon" | OpenAI, June 10, 2026 | Data-center electricity costs | Two account clusters | "no evidence of meaningful breakout beyond its own activity" |
| Suspected Chinese bot farm | X, Aug 27, 2026 | Opposition to AI data centers | About 200 of ~200,000 accounts | None asserted |

### What the discoverers say it did

OpenAI, in the same report: the operation "is significant not because the
operation appears to have shifted public opinion, but because it shows
PRC-origin influence operators testing narratives." It "sought to exploit
and amplify existing public concerns about energy prices and local impacts
of data center development, but we found no evidence of meaningful breakout
beyond its own activity." And: "Foreign influence operations have long
sought to latch onto existing local issues and sincerely held beliefs."

Deseret's own sourcing cut both ways. A former House Intelligence Committee
member who now runs an AI-safeguards group said "It's not just 200; it's
probably 200,000." The federal-affairs director of a pro-AI think tank said
the opposite: "It's overstating the fact to say that the social media debate
is driven by the Chinese Communist Party."

### What the intelligence community says

The March 2026 Annual Threat Assessment of the U.S. Intelligence Community,
the government's own public statement of foreign threats, was searched in
full. It does not mention data centers. It contains no finding of a Chinese
influence operation aimed at Americans' attitudes toward AI. Its AI section
describes a technology competition: "For technology powers such as the U.S.
and China, AI and quantum information science... are at the center of this
leadership competition."

### What Americans' "fear factor" looks like, measured

Pew Research Center, survey of June 22–28, 2026: "52% of Americans say they
are more concerned than excited about the increased use of AI in daily life
– up from 37% in 2021. Another 9% are more excited than concerned and 37%
say they're equally excited and concerned." The rise runs across every age
group and began before either operation above existed.

**In plain language:** Two companies caught Chinese accounts pushing one
argument, that data centers raise your power bill. One of the companies
says it found no sign the accounts moved anyone. The government's threat
assessment does not mention it. Meanwhile, half of the country says AI
worries it more than it excites them, a share that has been climbing since
2021. He described the whole of that worry as a Chinese propaganda campaign
and, in the earlier clip, said it was "funded."

### What he offered as his basis

Nothing, in either clip. The second time he said "I believe." The first
time he said "we don't even know it," which is not a description of
evidence.

## The fair reading

He is not inventing the campaign. Chinese-origin accounts did target one
AI debate in the United States this year, the platforms that found them
said so publicly, and a senator and a former intelligence-committee member
have drawn the same broad conclusion he did. "Moon race" and "Cold War" are
opinion, and the record takes no position on them. His claim that
established AI companies favor regulation that would exclude smaller rivals
is a live argument in the industry and is not something a record can
settle.

What the record does not support is the leap from "200 accounts about
power bills" to "the fear factor... is part of a Chinese propaganda
campaign to try to scare the American people." The evidence is about data
centers and tariffs, not about AI itself; its discoverers say it did not
break out; the intelligence community has not said it exists; and the
public concern he attributes to it is measured, broad and older than the
operation. He sits on the committee that could ask the agencies for a
classified answer. He has not said that he did.

## Questions this raises

1. What source, briefing or report is the basis for the statement that
   public fear of AI is "part of a Chinese propaganda campaign"?
2. Does he distinguish between the documented operation, which concerned
   data-center electricity costs, and public concern about AI generally?
3. Has he asked the Office of the Director of National Intelligence, the
   FBI or CISA whether such a campaign exists, and what did they say?
4. Given Pew's finding that 52% of Americans are more concerned than
   excited about AI, does he regard that share of his constituents as
   propaganda victims, and how does that view shape his votes on AI
   preemption?
5. Which "big superpowers within AI" does he say are feeding propaganda to
   secure regulation that excludes competitors?
