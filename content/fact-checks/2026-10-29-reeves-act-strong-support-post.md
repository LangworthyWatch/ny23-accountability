## Why this matters

For a disabled adult in the Southern Tier, the difference between living
at home and living in a nursing home is usually a Medicaid program called
home- and community-based services. On June 18, 2026, the Justice Department's Office of Legal
Counsel said federal disability law does not require states to serve
disabled people in "the most integrated setting." On September 23 a
federal court in Texas, at the Justice Department's own request, struck
the Health and Human Services rules that said it does. The Latonya Reeves
Freedom Act, introduced June 23, is Congress's answer: it would write the
right to community-based services into statute.

On October 27, disability advocates rallied outside Rep. Langworthy's
Elmira office and thanked him for his "strong support." The official page
shared the praise the next day. The congressional record shows what the
support is: he added his name to the bill on October 22, four months
after it was introduced, two days before early voting opened, and five
days before the rally. He has said nothing in public, as far as this site
can find, about the Justice Department reversal the bill responds to. And
in 2025 he voted twice for the law that makes the largest cut in
Medicaid's history, the program that pays for the services the bill is
meant to protect.

## What the bill is, and why it exists now

**The bill.** H.R. 9401, the Latonya Reeves Freedom Act of 2026, was
introduced June 23, 2026, by Rep. Steve Cohen (D-Tenn.) with 48
cosponsors. Its stated purposes include "to affirm that every individual
who is eligible for long-term services and supports has a federally
protected right to be integrated into that individual's community and
receive community-based long-term services and supports," and "to
clarify and strengthen the integration mandate of the Americans with
Disabilities Act of 1990, held by the Supreme Court in Olmstead v. L.C."
It was referred to Energy and Commerce, where Rep. Langworthy sits, and
to Judiciary. It has not had a hearing.

**Why June 23.** Five days earlier, on June 18, the Justice Department's
Office of Legal Counsel issued an opinion that, in NPR's description, said
the department no longer interpreted disability law to "require states to
treat mentally disabled patients in the most integrated setting
appropriate to their needs," and acknowledged its position was "out of
step with the common understanding" of the Supreme Court's 1999 Olmstead
decision. In July the department stopped relying on its own enforcement
guidance for the integration mandate. Then it switched sides in a lawsuit
that seventeen states had brought against the Biden administration's
Section 504 rules. On September 23, 2026, the U.S. District Court for the
Northern District of Texas, in Texas v. Kennedy, granted a joint motion
from the Justice Department and the states and vacated the HHS
regulations requiring services "in the most integrated setting," nationwide.
The Americans with Disabilities Act and the Olmstead decision itself were
not changed.

Bruce Darling, president of the Center for Disability Rights, the
organization that ran the Elmira rally, told NPR the ruling felt "like a
knife in your chest. Because what we're hearing, what people are hearing,
is that the federal government has made a determination that [disabled
people] don't have a right to live in freedom, that they can be locked up
against their will." His organization, NPR reported, "is putting its
energy behind a proposed law that would cement protections around
integration." That law is H.R. 9401. On June 24 a Senate resolution
called on the department to rescind the opinion; House Democrats' ranking
members on three committees wrote to the acting attorney general asking
the same.

## What the post leaves out

**1. His votes for the law that cuts the program the bill protects.**
Rep. Langworthy voted Aye on H.R. 1, the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, at
both stages: May 22, 2025 (Roll Call 145) and July 3, 2025 (Roll Call
190, 218-214). The Congressional Budget Office estimated the law's
Medicaid provisions reduce federal Medicaid spending by $840 billion over
ten years and that 10 million people lose health coverage by 2034,
including 1.3 million people enrolled in both Medicare and Medicaid, the
group that includes most disabled adults and seniors in long-term care.
Medicaid is the dominant payer for home- and community-based services.

The National Health Law Program's October 2025 analysis of the law's
effect on people with disabilities lists what takes effect: work
requirements for Medicaid expansion adults ages 19 to 64 starting
January 1, 2027, with exemptions on paper for people who are "medically
frail," receive services through a home- and community-based waiver, or
care for a disabled family member; eligibility redeterminations every six
months instead of twelve, which the analysis says "increases risk of
procedural terminations" and "increases burden on people with
disabilities"; shortened retroactive coverage; copays of up to $35 per
service for expansion adults starting October 2028; and a freeze on the
provider taxes states use to fund the program, which the analysis says
"will impact HCBS funding." The law also included a ten-year block on the
federal nursing-home minimum-staffing rule.

The American Association of People with Disabilities, whose
representatives he met on May 18, 2026, responded to the law's passage
with a statement titled "This Is A Devastating Day for Disabled
Americans." This site has documented that meeting and the vote
[here](/fact-checks/2026-05-18-aapd-medicaid-disability-contradiction/).

Providers in the district were saying so before the law's provisions
took effect. Suburban Adult Services, Inc., headquartered in Elma in the
district's Erie County portion, serves nearly 2,000 people with
intellectual and developmental disabilities across the state. Its
president, Jeff Paterson, told Spectrum News in August 2025 that
inflation was already stretching the agency's budget, that "it might mean
that you can't go out and do as many things in the community," and that
"on top of inflation, cuts to SNAP and Medicaid are also an issue."

The Reeves Act would guarantee a right to receive long-term services in
the community. H.R. 1 cuts the money that pays for them. A reader of the
post would not know he voted for the second before signing the first.

**2. No public word on the reversal the bill answers.** This site
searched his office's press-release index (70 releases, August 25, 2025,
through October 15, 2026), his X timeline for October 16 through 27, and
the web for any statement by him on the June 18 opinion, the July
guidance withdrawal, or the September 23 court order. None was found. The
press index contains no release on any disability topic in that period;
the word "accessibility" appears once, in a release about a community
center. He is a member of the House Oversight Committee. His Facebook
posts and radio appearances were not systematically searched, so this is
a statement about where this site looked, not proof he never spoke.

**3. The disability bills he has not joined.** The same cosponsor records
show he is not on:

- **H.R. 2598, the IDEA Full Funding Act** (introduced April 2, 2025),
  which would phase federal special-education funding up to the 40 percent
  share Congress promised in 1975. It has 175 cosponsors, including 16
  Republicans, among them three fellow New York Republicans: Lawler,
  Garbarino and LaLota.
- **H.R. 6617, the Keeping All Students Safe Act** (December 11, 2025),
  which would limit the restraint and seclusion of students. This site has
  documented rising restraint counts in NY-23 schools
  [here](/fact-checks/2026-06-14-ocr-collapse-disabled-students/).
- **H.R. 10232** (September 2, 2026), a bipartisan bill by Reps. John
  Mannion (D-N.Y.), Bobby Scott (D-Va.) and Brian Fitzpatrick (R-Pa.) to
  bar the Education Department from transferring its special-education
  office to other agencies. On June 16, 2026, the department announced an
  agreement handing administration of grants under the Individuals with
  Disabilities Education Act to Health and Human Services, and moving
  disability-discrimination complaint handling to the Justice Department.
  Rep. Langworthy has endorsed "streamlining" the department and
  "bringing it closer to home."

**4. The other pending cut to disabled adults who live at home.** The
Social Security Administration listed in its Spring 2025 regulatory
agenda a rule to rescind the 2024 definition of a "public assistance
household," which would again count a bedroom in a relative's home as
income against a disabled adult's SSI check even when the household is
poor enough for SNAP. ProPublica reported on April 28, 2026, that White
House and Department of Government Efficiency officials were still
pushing the change, that it could cut or end benefits for as many as
400,000 recipients, and that the cut would be up to a third of the check,
about $330 a month in the case it profiled. As of October 29, 2026, no
proposed rule has been published in the Federal Register and the item
has not reappeared in later agendas. No statement from him on it was
located.

## The fair reading

Cosponsoring the Reeves Act is a real act, and it puts him on the right
side of a bill that most of his party has not joined. The advocates who
thanked him were thanking him for something he actually did. Signing on
late is still signing on; one Republican joined after him.

The SSI cosponsorship is also real, and that bill has broad Republican
support. He signed the New York delegation's 2023 letter against Medicare
home-health payment cuts. He voted for the FY2026 spending law that
funded special education and the Education Department's civil-rights
office at roughly flat levels, and for the Social Security Fairness Act
in November 2024. He is not a cosponsor of the bill to abolish the
Education Department, nor of the ACCESS Act that disability groups oppose
because it would require notice before an ADA lawsuit.

H.R. 1's work requirements do exempt people with disabilities in the
statute's text; the documented concern of disability lawyers is not the
exemption but the paperwork that will cost eligible people their
coverage. And nothing in this entry suggests his October 22 signature was
anything other than sincere.

What the post omits is the sequence. The reversal came in June. The bill
came five days later. Most of its Republican cosponsors signed by
September. His signature came the week early voting opened, after a year
in which he voted twice for the law that cuts the program the bill
protects and said nothing, that this site can find, about the Justice
Department's change of position. The advocates were "heartened" by
strong support. The record shows recent support.

## Questions this raises

1. What changed between June 23, when the bill was introduced, and
   October 22, when he signed it?
2. Does he agree with the Justice Department's June 18 opinion that
   federal law does not require states to serve disabled people in the
   most integrated setting? If not, why has he not said so?
3. How does he reconcile a cosponsorship guaranteeing community-based
   long-term services with two votes for a law that the Congressional
   Budget Office says removes $840 billion from the program that funds
   them?
4. Three fellow New York Republicans cosponsored the IDEA Full Funding
   Act eighteen months ago. Why not him?
