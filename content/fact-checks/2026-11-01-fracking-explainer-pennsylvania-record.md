## Why this matters

The Southern Tier sits on the northern edge of the Marcellus Shale, the
formation that made Pennsylvania the country's second-largest natural gas
producer. New York has not allowed the drilling method that unlocked it.
That difference is now a campaign issue: the Republican nominee for
governor says he will overturn the ban, the EPA administrator came to
upstate New York in June to urge the same, and Rep. Langworthy has made
the comparison with Pennsylvania a theme of his energy messaging for two
years.

In December 2024 he wrote that Pennsylvania "transformed its rural
economies," that drilling there led to "growing populations," and that
the difference between the border counties "have often been likened to
East and West Berlin." In October 2026 his campaign page captioned a
debate clip: "Pennsylvania prospers. Our great state suffers."

This entry explains what fracking is, what New York banned and when, and
then lets the Pennsylvania record speak: production, money, jobs,
population, prices, and the health and regulatory findings Pennsylvania's
own institutions have published. The border-county numbers are the test
of his claim.

## Part 1: What fracking is, and what New York banned

**The method.** Shale gas sits in rock too tight to flow on its own.
Operators drill down a mile or more, turn the bore horizontal for
thousands of feet, then pump water, sand and chemical additives in at
high pressure to crack the rock and prop the fractures open. "High-volume
hydraulic fracturing," the term in New York law, means a well using
300,000 gallons of water or more. A single Marcellus well typically uses
millions.

**The timeline in New York.**
- **2008.** The Department of Environmental Conservation begins a
  supplemental environmental review of high-volume fracturing and issues
  no high-volume permits while it runs.
- **December 2014.** The Department of Health completes a public health
  review. Its conclusion, in the acting commissioner's words: "the overall
  weight of the evidence from the cumulative body of information contained
  in this Public Health Review demonstrates that there are significant
  uncertainties about the kinds of adverse health outcomes that may be
  associated with HVHF, the likelihood of the occurrence of adverse health
  outcomes, and the effectiveness of some of the mitigation measures in
  reducing or preventing environmental impacts which could adversely
  affect public health. Until the science provides sufficient information
  to determine the level of risk to public health from HVHF to all New
  Yorkers and whether the risks can be adequately managed, DOH recommends
  that HVHF should not proceed in NYS."
- **June 2015.** The Department of Environmental Conservation issues its
  findings statement: high-volume hydraulic fracturing "will be prohibited
  in New York State."
- **April 2020.** The Legislature writes the prohibition into statute in
  the state budget, Environmental Conservation Law section 23-0501(3).
- **December 21, 2024.** After a Texas company, Southern Tier Solutions,
  mailed lease offers to landowners in Tioga, Broome and Chemung counties
  proposing to fracture the shale with liquid carbon dioxide instead of
  water, Gov. Hochul signs a bill adding "and carbon dioxide" to the ban.

**What is not banned.** Conventional, low-volume drilling remains legal.
As the earlier entry documented from state well records, the DEC issued
52 oil-well development permits in 2024 and 58 in 2025. New York still
produces gas from older wells, about 8.8 billion cubic feet in 2025, down
from a peak of 56 billion in 2006.

**What lifting the ban would take.** Because the prohibition is in
statute, a governor cannot lift it alone. It would take a bill passed by
both houses of the Legislature and signed by the governor, followed by
new DEC rules and permits. Gov. Hochul said in June 2026: "I don't have
any guarantee that the technology has gotten any better or more secure. I've
not seen that."

## Part 2: What Pennsylvania got

**Production.** In 2008 Pennsylvania produced 198 billion cubic feet of
gas. In 2019 it produced 6,897 billion. In 2025, 7,676 billion, about 16
percent of all the gas produced in the United States and roughly 870
times what New York produced. Output has been flat since 2020, moving
between 7.2 and 7.7 trillion cubic feet a year; the state's utility
commission attributes the year-to-year swing in new wells to the price of
gas.

**Money to governments.** Pennsylvania does not tax gas production. It
charges a per-well impact fee under its 2012 drilling law. For 2025 the
Public Utility Commission distributed $243.9 million, of which $133.8
million went to the counties and municipalities where drilling happens
and $89.2 million to a statewide environmental fund. The cumulative total
since 2012 is $3.12 billion. The 2025 figure was $79 million higher than
2024 because operators drilled 444 new wells instead of 309 and the gas
price rose from $2.27 to $3.43 per million BTU. The fee rises and falls
with the market.

**Jobs.** The Bureau of Labor Statistics counts, for 2024, 3,311 private
jobs in oil and gas extraction in Pennsylvania, 1,021 in drilling wells,
and 6,848 in support activities for oil and gas operations, about 11,200
in all, at average pay between $100,000 and $144,000. That is about
two-tenths of one percent of the state's 6.0 million jobs.
Industry-funded studies produce far larger numbers by counting pipeline
construction, trucking, suppliers, and the spending of workers' wages. A
Penn State economist who co-authored a 2016 study of the industry's
employment told WHYY in 2024 that "typically anything over [twice] the
number of direct jobs is looked at as unreasonable by economists who do
this type of economic impact analysis." The 11,200 figure is the direct
count. New York's equivalent is 565.

**Prices.** The earlier entry reported federal data for 2025: residential
natural gas cost $17.58 per thousand cubic feet in New York and $15.04 in
Pennsylvania, and residential electricity 26.39 cents per kilowatt-hour
against 19.30. Pennsylvania's energy is cheaper. How much of the gap is
drilling, how much is New York City's utility rates, taxes, and delivery
costs, the data do not say; the same entry found the gap for the
utilities serving NY-23 was 6 to 25 percent, not 37.

**Landowners.** Royalty payments to Pennsylvania landowners who leased
their mineral rights are private and not reported in aggregate by the
state; they are real income for the families who hold leases and are not
quantified here.

## Part 3: The border counties, side by side

Rep. Langworthy's claim is specifically about the counties on either side
of the line. Here they are, using the Census Bureau's population
estimates and the Bureau of Labor Statistics' county employment files.
Bradford, Susquehanna and Tioga counties in Pennsylvania are among the
most heavily drilled in the state and sit directly across the border from
Chemung, Steuben and Tioga counties in New York.

The 2014 baseline comes after the first years of the drilling boom, so
this table does not capture the boom itself; it shows where the
counties stand now. On that measure the two sides of the border are
indistinguishable. The three New York counties directly across from the
gas fields have higher average pay than their Pennsylvania neighbors, and
Tioga County, New York, is the only county in the table that added jobs.
County-level gas-industry employment is suppressed in the federal data
for most of these counties to protect employer confidentiality, so the
direct drilling jobs cannot be separated out at the county level.

**In plain language:** the numbers do not show a Pennsylvania side that
grew and a New York side that emptied. Both sides shrank at about the
same rate, and the New York counties across from the gas fields pay more.

## Part 4: What it cost

**The state's own grand jury.** In June 2020, after a two-year
investigation, Pennsylvania's 43rd Statewide Investigating Grand Jury
issued a 235-page report on the unconventional gas industry and the
agencies that regulate it. On the Department of Health: "our government
-- including its Department of Health (DOH) -- does not recognize that
fracking operations harm public health, citing insufficient research on
the issue. However, the absence of such research, at least in part, is due
to DOH's own failure to inquire into the matter over the past decade. This
'wait and see' approach facilitates placing the health risks of the shale
gas industry's operations on everyday Pennsylvanians. We find this status
quo unacceptable." The jurors made eight recommendations. The first: "The closer
people happen to live to a massive, industrial drilling complex, the worse
it is likely to be for them," and the legal minimum distance from a home,
500 feet, "just isn't high enough." It recommended 2,500 feet, and 5,000
from schools and hospitals. The second: "Stop the Chemical Cover-up,"
because "under existing law, the oil and gas companies don't have to say
what chemicals they are using until after they have already used them."
The attorney general who convened it, Josh Shapiro, is now Pennsylvania's
governor.

**Dimock.** In Susquehanna County, methane from Cabot Oil & Gas wells
migrated into residents' water wells; one exploded in January 2009. The company long denied wrongdoing. On November 29, 2022,
its successor, Coterra Energy, pleaded no contest to environmental crimes
and agreed to pay $16.29 million for a public water line and 75 years of
residents' water bills.

**The health studies.** In August 2023 the University of Pittsburgh
released three studies commissioned by Pennsylvania's Department of
Health, covering eight southwestern counties and health records from
2010 to 2020. As summarized in DEP's own review of the findings: children
living within one mile of a well had "approximately 5 to 7 x chance of
developing lymphoma compared with unexposed (>5 miles) children," with no
association found for leukemia, brain or bone cancers; people with asthma
had a "4-5 x greater chance of asthma attack if within 10 miles" during
the production phase, with "no such link during well preparation,
drilling or fracking phases"; and babies born to mothers living near
producing wells were about one ounce smaller, an effect the reviewers
said "in most cases, poses little health risk." These are statistical
associations from observational studies. DEP's reviewers listed the
"specific cause(s) of correlations" as an open question, and the studies
do not claim to have answered it.

**The wells left behind.** The Department of Environmental Protection
estimates that "hundreds of thousands of oil and gas wells have been
drilled in Pennsylvania since 1859" and that abandoned and orphan wells
"pose a risk to environmental and human health, including the potential
for methane leaks, migration, and water contamination." Most predate
fracking; the shale wells now being drilled will join them when they stop
producing.

## The fair reading

Pennsylvania's gas is real, large, and cheap, and it has paid for things:
a quarter-billion dollars a year to local governments and state programs,
more than 11,000 direct jobs at good wages, royalties to the families who
leased, and lower utility bills across the state. The health findings are
associations in observational studies, not proof, and the industry
disputes them. The grand jury's harshest findings were about regulators,
not drillers, and the drilling went on. Nothing in the record says New
York's economy would not have gained something from the same industry.

What the record does not support is the picture he painted. The
Pennsylvania counties across the border from NY-23 did not grow; they
shrank faster than some of their New York neighbors. Their job counts fell
at the same rate. Their average pay is lower. The drilling jobs the
industry created are a fraction of one percent of Pennsylvania's
workforce. And the study he called bogus reached the same place
Pennsylvania's own grand jury and health department researchers reached
years later: that the risks to people living near wells were real enough
to warrant distance and disclosure, which Pennsylvania still has not
fully required.

The honest version of the comparison is this: Pennsylvania chose the gas
and its costs, New York chose neither, and the border counties on both
sides are losing people either way.

## Update, November 1, 2026: the New York Post repeats the claim, and he shares it

On October 29 the New York Post's editorial board endorsed the state's
Republican House incumbents. Its paragraph on Rep. Langworthy:

> "Nick Langworthy, repping the Southern Tier and the Buffalo suburbs in
> the 23rd CD, backs strong immigration enforcement and US energy
> independence. He's rightly a relentless critic of Gov. Kathy Hochul's
> feckless economic policies, not least the foolish ban on fracking, a
> technique that has enriched next-door counties in Pennsylvania."

His campaign page posted the paragraph as a graphic on October 30 with
the caption: "I'm proud to earn the endorsement from the New York Post.
I'll never stop fighting for secure borders, American energy
independence, and an economy that respects hardworking New Yorkers. While
Kathy Hochul holds our state back, I'll keep fighting to move us forward."

Two points from the record above. First, the ban is not Gov. Hochul's;
it was imposed by the state's health and environmental agencies in 2014
and 2015 and written into law in April 2020, before she took office.
Second, "enriched next-door counties" is the claim the border-county
tables test. The next-door counties, Bradford, Susquehanna and Tioga,
lost 4.8, 12.0 and 2.9 percent of their population from 2010 to 2024 and
3 to 9 percent of their private jobs from 2014 to 2024, and their average
pay in 2024 was below that of Chemung, Steuben and Tioga counties in New
York directly across the line. The impact fees those counties received
are real and are reported above; the enrichment the editorial describes
does not show up in the people, the jobs or the paychecks.
