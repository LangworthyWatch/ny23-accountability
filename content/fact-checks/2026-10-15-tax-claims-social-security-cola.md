## Why this matters

The Social Security Administration announced the 2027 cost-of-living
adjustment on October 14; nearly 72 million beneficiaries will see it. The
same day, Rep. Langworthy told WGRZ that the tax law he voted for
"eliminated taxes on Social Security for middle class seniors." He made
the same claim at his October 6 debate, alongside several other claims
about the law.

Some of what he said is accurate. The central claim about Social Security
is not: the law created a temporary deduction for most taxpayers 65 and
over, and left the rules for taxing Social Security benefits exactly as they
were. Seniors deciding how to plan for next year's taxes should know the
difference.

## Claim 1: "Eliminated taxes on Social Security for middle class seniors"

**Verdict: FALSE**

How much of a Social Security benefit is taxable is set by Section 86 of
the Internal Revenue Code. The 2025 law did not amend it. The Congressional
Research Service, in its report on the law's senior provisions: the
taxable-benefit determination is in Section 86 of the Code "and was not
changed by P.L. 119-21."

What the law did instead, in Section 70103, was create a new deduction of
$6,000 for each taxpayer age 65 or older, for tax years 2025 through 2028.
It is not tied to Social Security. CRS notes it is "not limited to Social
Security beneficiaries," and a 65-year-old with no benefits gets it too. It
shrinks by 6 percent of income above $75,000 ($150,000 for joint filers),
reaching zero at $175,000 ($250,000 joint).

Who it misses:

- **Beneficiaries under 65.** As of December 2023, 20.2 percent of
  Social Security recipients, 13.6 million people, were under 65 (CRS,
  from SSA data). They are not eligible for the deduction themselves
  (one married to a spouse 65 or older can benefit through the spouse's
  deduction on a joint return).
- **Seniors with low incomes.** A deduction only helps someone with taxable
  income. CRS: it "is not a tax credit, so it by itself will not create a
  tax refund."
- **Seniors whose income is still taxable after the deduction.** The Tax
  Policy Center estimated that the deduction "would benefit fewer than half
  of older adults," and that about half of all Social Security recipients
  "will pay at least some income taxes on their benefits." Its summary:
  "most would see their income taxes on Social Security benefits reduced,
  not eliminated."

The Social Security Administration made a similar claim in a July 3, 2025
release ("nearly 90% of Social Security beneficiaries will no longer pay
federal income taxes on their benefits"). The Tax Policy Center called
that claim untrue. The site has rated Rep. Langworthy's earlier versions
of the claim MISLEADING twice and, in one sub-claim, FALSE (see Related).

The FALSE rating here rests on the categorical word: "eliminated taxes on
Social Security for middle class seniors." The debate slogan "no tax on
Social Security" makes the same claim in shorter form.

In plain language: the law gave many seniors a tax cut. It did not stop
Social Security benefits from being taxable, for middle-class seniors or
anyone else, and beneficiaries under 65 are not eligible for it.

## Claim 2: "$6,000 more relief"

**Verdict: MISLEADING**

The $6,000 is the size of the deduction, not the amount of tax saved. A
deduction lowers taxable income; the saving is the deduction times the
taxpayer's tax rate. For a single senior who can use all of it:

| Federal tax bracket | Tax saved, one senior | Tax saved, couple both 65+ |
|---|---|---|
| 10% | $600 | $1,200 |
| 12% | $720 | $1,440 |
| 22% | $1,320 | $2,640 |
| No taxable income | $0 | $0 |

It phases out at higher incomes, as described above, and expires after
2028.

The post sets "$6,000 more relief" next to "$75 more a month" from the
COLA. The COLA is cash: $75 a month works out to $900 a year for the
average retiree. The $6,000 is not cash. The Tax Policy Center estimated
that the seniors who gain the most, those making about $80,000 to
$130,000, would see an average tax cut of about $1,100.

In plain language: for a single senior in the 10 to 22 percent brackets
who can use all of it, the deduction is worth $600 to $1,320 a year.
It is worth nothing to those who owe no income tax. It is not $6,000.

## Claim 3: "A 3.6% COLA means $75 more a month"

**Verdict: TRUE**

The Social Security Administration announced on October 14, 2026: "a 3.6
percent benefit increase for 2027" and "On average, Social Security
retirement benefits will increase by $75 per month." Both of his figures
match. His statement also correctly notes that benefits "have been
adjusted for inflation annually for decades": the adjustment is automatic
under the Social Security Act, tied to the Consumer Price Index for Urban
Wage Earners and Clerical Workers.

(WGRZ described the COLA as "projected." SSA had announced it earlier the
same day.)

## Claims 4 and 5: The Child Tax Credit

**"We doubled the child tax credit": MISLEADING**

The credit was doubled, from $1,000 to $2,000, by Section 11022 of the Tax
Cuts and Jobs Act ("substituting `$2,000' for `$1,000'"), which was
approved on December 22, 2017. That was before Rep. Langworthy took his
seat: the House Clerk's records show him answering the first roll call of
the new Congress on January 3, 2023.

The law he voted for, Section 70104 of P.L. 119-21, struck "$2,000" and
inserted "$2,200": a 10 percent increase. It also made the credit
permanent instead of letting it fall back to $1,000 after 2025, and it
added a requirement that the taxpayer (or at least one spouse on a joint
return) have a work-eligible Social Security number. The child's number
was already required under the 2017 law.

The fair reading: "we" in a debate can mean his party, and Republicans did
pass the 2017 doubling. But in an answer defending the law he voted for,
"we doubled the child tax credit" credits that law with something it did
not do. Keeping the doubled credit from expiring is a real change and a
fair claim. Doubling it is not.

**"A larger Child Tax Credit": TRUE.** $2,000 to $2,200, as above.

**"Expanded tax relief for seniors": TRUE** as worded: the $6,000
deduction described in Claim 1.

**"A higher standard deduction": TRUE.** Section 70102 raised it to
$15,750 for single filers for 2025 ($31,500 for joint filers, per CRS) and
removed the 2025 expiration.

## Claim 6: "No tax on tips or overtime"

**Verdict: MISLEADING**

"No Tax on Tips" and "No Tax on Overtime" are the provisions' popular
names, used across parties and in the press. The label still overstates
what they do: both are deductions, not exemptions, and both are capped and
temporary. The site rated the same claim MISLEADING in
[April](/fact-checks/2026-04-07-wftc-refund-claims/).
From the IRS fact sheet and the statute:

| | Tips (Sec. 70201) | Overtime (Sec. 70202) |
|---|---|---|
| Maximum deduction | $25,000 | $12,500 ($25,000 joint) |
| Phases out above | $150,000 ($300,000 joint) | $150,000 ($300,000 joint) |
| What counts | Tips in occupations the IRS lists as customarily tipped | Only the premium portion, "such as the 'half' portion of 'time-and-a-half'" |
| Years | 2025 through 2028 | 2025 through 2028 |

Married filers must file jointly and include a Social Security number to
claim either one.

In plain language: many tipped and hourly workers will pay less income
tax. "No tax" overstates it: these are capped income-tax deductions, the
base pay for overtime is still taxed, and both breaks end after 2028
unless Congress extends them.

---

## Claim 7: "Tax refund checks went up 11%"

**Verdict: TRUE**

IRS filing-season statistics through May 8, 2026: the average refund was
$3,276, compared with $2,939 at the same point in 2025, up 11.5 percent.
The figure is a national average; the IRS filing-season statistics are
national only, so "right here in our community" is an inference.
The site's [April entry](/fact-checks/2026-04-07-wftc-refund-claims/) covers
what else drove larger refunds, including the higher cap on the state and
local tax deduction, and why a larger refund is not the same thing as a
larger tax cut.

## Questions this raises

1. Which middle-class seniors did the law "eliminate taxes on Social
   Security" for, given that Section 86 of the tax code was not changed?
2. What does he tell the roughly one in five Social Security beneficiaries
   who are under 65 and are not eligible for the senior deduction?
3. Why present a $6,000 deduction alongside a $75-a-month cash increase as
   though both were money in hand?
