## Why this matters

When Russia invaded Ukraine in 2022, Nick Langworthy, then chairman of the
New York Republican Party, called it "a dark day for the democratic world"
and blamed "Biden's weakness." He said "every moment that goes by without
severe consequences is a threat to the entire globe." In Congress he kept
the theme: "American weakness invites aggression."

On October 16, 2026, the President used a waiver in this year's Russia
sanctions law, which Rep. Langworthy voted for, to set aside the law's new
tariffs and most of its other sanctions. That week Ukraine also lost
some access to U.S. satellite services, which anonymous officials told
Reuters was ordered from inside the White House, an account the
administration denies.

Rep. Langworthy sits on the House Oversight Committee. This entry lays out what he has said and how
he has voted, and what we could find of his response this month.

## The waiver he voted for, and how it was used

**What the law allows.** Section 115 of the 2026 sanctions law says: "The
President may, subject to subsection (b), waive the application of any
sanctions provision with respect to a foreign person, any restriction with
respect to a person, or any duty under this title." Before doing so, the
President must send Congress "a certification in writing that the issuance
of the waiver is in the national interests of the United States" and a
report explaining it. Unlike terminations under Section 117, a waiver
under Section 115 is not subject to a congressional resolution of
disapproval.

**A chance to tighten it.** At the Rules Committee meeting on the bill on
September 14, 2026, Rep. Meeks's amendment to allow waivers only when
"vital to the national security of the United States" was defeated 3 to 7.
Rep. Langworthy, a member of the committee, is recorded as not voting.

**How it was used.** On October 16, 2026, the President signed an order
invoking Section 115 to "waive the application of any duties provided for
in sections 112 and 113 of the Graham Act," including the law's tariffs on
Russian goods and on top buyers of Russian oil, along with most of its
other sanctions provisions. The order says the waivers remain in effect
until the President or Secretary of State "determine that further action
is appropriate." It says the required certification and report were sent
to Congress; they have not been made public. Earlier sanctions under prior
executive orders remain in place.

**The Starshield reports.** Ukrainian forces lost some speed and access on
the military Starshield satellite network around October 13-14. Reuters
reported, citing two U.S. officials and a Western official speaking
anonymously, that the order to limit it "came from inside the White
House." A senior U.S. official told Reuters "No such request was ever made
by the president," and Secretary of State Rubio called the account
"categorically false." The Hill reported full access was restored on
October 14; President Zelensky said on October 19 that speeds were still
capped in places. These are reports, not established facts.

**Who has asked questions.** Four Republican senators, Susan Collins, John
Cornyn, Lisa Murkowski and Thom Tillis, joined Democrats in asking the
Pentagon's inspector general to investigate whether officials "directed"
or "authorized" the changes, The Hill reported on October 16.

**What we found from him.** As of October 19, we found no statement from
Rep. Langworthy on the waiver or the Starshield reports in his press
releases or his recent posts on X. We could not fully check his Facebook
pages while logged out, so this is a partial check.

## Where the public is

An Economist/YouGov poll taken April 17-20, 2026 found 62 percent of
Americans sympathize more with Ukraine and 2 percent with Russia. A YouGov
survey on October 14, 2026 found 57 percent sympathize more with Ukraine,
57 percent support existing sanctions on Russian oil exports, and 40
percent oppose suspending sanctions on Russian diesel, against 33 percent
who support it.

## The fair reading, at full strength

- **He has explained his aid votes.** In April 2024 he said he opposed the
  $60 billion bill because it included $7.85 billion for Ukrainian
  government salaries and pensions and a $9.85 billion loan the President
  could forgive, among other reasons, and that he would have supported a
  munitions-only bill with oversight.
- **He voted for tough sanctions.** He voted for the 2026 sanctions law,
  and in November 2025 he cosponsored a Russia sanctions bill (H.R. 2548)
  whose only waiver, as introduced, was a one-time tariff waiver of up to
  180 days.
- **The 2022 statement came before he was in Congress,** as a party
  chairman criticizing a president of the other party.
- **Silence is not a vote.** Members do not comment on every action, and
  he may have spoken in places we could not check.
- **The Starshield accounts are disputed** and come from anonymous sources.

What the record shows is a change in emphasis: sharp public criticism of
"weakness" toward Russia under the last administration, and no public
response we could find, from a member of the Oversight Committee, when the
current administration waived the sanctions he voted for.

## Questions this raises

1. The law requires the President to send Congress a written certification
   and a report explaining the waiver. Has Rep. Langworthy read them, and
   will he ask for them to be made public?
2. In 2022 he said every moment without severe consequences was "a threat
   to the entire globe." Does he consider the October 16 waiver consistent
   with that?
3. Why did he not vote on the Rules Committee amendment that would have
   narrowed the waiver?
4. Will he join the four Republican senators in asking for an inspector
   general review of the Starshield restrictions?
