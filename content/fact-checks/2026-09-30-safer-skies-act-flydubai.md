## Why this matters

A frightening event is the moment a member of Congress is most likely to be
believed. On the morning a Flydubai pilot stabbed his colleague at the
controls and 174 people nearly died, Rep. Langworthy told his constituents
that passing his bill would keep "our planes" from ever again being used as
a weapon. His bill exists, it is bipartisan, and it addresses a real gap.
The gap it addresses is not the one on that flight.

## The record

### What happened on the flight

Flydubai flight FZ1073, a Boeing 737 MAX 8 from Dubai to Tel Aviv with 174
aboard. Per NBC News, "Flight data indicates the Boeing 737, flight number
FZ 1073, plunged by 14,125 feet in just 29 seconds, according to
Flightradar24." One pilot stabbed the other in the cockpit. The stabbed
captain, Smit Machchhar, opened the cockpit door; passengers and crew
"rush[ed] in, secure[d] the cockpit and prevent[ed] the plane from
crashing." The aircraft landed at Tabuk, Saudi Arabia, where the attacking
pilot was taken into custody.

On motive, the parties said different things on the same day:

| Who | What they said (Sept 30) |
|---|---|
| Israeli Defense Minister Israel Katz | "an attempted jihadist terrorist attack," per NPR "without providing details" |
| Prime Minister's spokesperson Doron Spielman | "there is a strong opinion" it was a terror attack, "but we can't be sure until we debrief everybody" (NBC) |
| Flydubai | "the underlying reasons and motives behind this event are unknown and remain subject to a formal investigation" (CBC, NPR) |
| UAE civil aviation authority | investigating the "causes and circumstances of the incident" (NPR) |

The attacker was not a passenger. He was one of the two people the
airline had put at the controls.

### What the Safer Skies Act does

H.R. 2353, the Safer Skies Act of 2025, introduced by Rep. Langworthy on
March 26, 2025. Its operative section, in full:

> "(a) In General.--Not later than 360 days after the date of the enactment
> of this Act, all covered air carrier operations shall be subject to
> section 1544.101(a) of title 49, Code of Federal Regulations (or successor
> regulation) (commonly known as the 'Aircraft Operator Standard Security
> Program')."

"Covered air carrier operations" are defined as operations that "(1)
operate under parts 135 and 380 of title 14, Code of Federal Regulations;
(2) conduct common carriage passenger-carrying operations for compensation
or hire for which the certificate holder... (A) offers individual seats in
advance; and (B) provides publicly available schedules...; (3) operate
airplanes with a passenger-seat configuration of more than nine seats; and
(4) do not enplane or deplane in a checkpoint managed by the Transportation
Security Administration."

His own August 5, 2024 release describing the bill: it "will close a
loophole that has allowed certain operators, like ticketed charter flights,
to bypass the rigorous screening requirements that ensure the safety of all
passengers." Specifically, it "would: Require the TSA to update its security
screening requirements for Part 135 and Part 380 operators that offer
individual seats in advance, provide publicly available schedules, and
operate passenger seat configurations of more than nine seats."

That is the whole bill. It moves scheduled U.S. public-charter passengers
through TSA checkpoint screening. It contains no provision on pilots,
crew vetting, cockpit access, flight-deck procedures, foreign carriers, or
flights outside the United States.

| The Flydubai event | The Safer Skies Act |
|---|---|
| Foreign airline, Dubai to Tel Aviv | U.S. Part 135/380 public-charter operations |
| Attacker was a pilot at the controls | Applies TSA passenger screening at checkpoints |
| Passengers stormed the cockpit to stop it | Silent on cockpit access and crew |
| Motive under investigation | Rationale: charters "bypass" passenger screening |

### The bill's status

Referred to the House Committee on Homeland Security and its Subcommittee
on Transportation and Maritime Security on March 26, 2025. As of the
govinfo status file updated September 25, 2026, that is the last recorded
action. It has 52 cosponsors, 26 Republicans and 26 Democrats. The House is
not in session until November 9.

### What the security field says the event illustrates

Philip Baum, managing director of an in-flight security consultancy, to
Al Jazeera: "Regardless of whether or not it was a terrorist attack or
whether it was a heated argument on the flight deck, or indeed whether it
was some mental health breakdown, it illustrates the fact that the insider
threat is probably the greatest threat to civil aviation nowadays." He
added: "In the post-9/11 era since September 11, 2001, we've actually lost
more lives on board commercial aircraft as a result of pilot suicide than
we have as a result of terrorism."

**In plain language:** The danger on that plane came from inside the
cockpit. His bill is about the line passengers stand in before they board a
charter flight in the United States. Passing it would not have changed who
was flying FZ1073 or what he could do once the door closed.

## The fair reading

The loophole his bill targets is real. Scheduled public charters that sell
seats to the public and skip TSA checkpoints are a documented concern, his
co-lead Rep. Veasey said in 2024 he was "troubled that a growing number of
public charter flight operators are trying to skirt TSA's security
standards," and the bill has drawn an even split of Republican and
Democratic cosponsors. He chairs the Aviation Safety Caucus, so aviation
security is a lane he works in, not one he picked up for the occasion.
"Attempted hijacking and terrorist attack" tracks what Israel's defense
minister said that morning, though not what the airline or the regulator
said.

What does not hold is the link he drew. A post that says "pass my Safer
Skies Act to ensure our planes can never again be used as a weapon of
terror," attached to a story about a pilot attacking his co-pilot on a
foreign carrier, tells constituents that his bill is the answer to what
they just read. Its text says otherwise. The event is an argument for
something, perhaps crew vetting or cockpit procedures. It is not an
argument for H.R. 2353.

## Questions this raises

1. Which provision of H.R. 2353 would have applied to a Flydubai pilot on a
   Dubai-to-Tel Aviv flight?
2. Does he support any measure on the insider threat, such as crew vetting
   or flight-deck procedures, and if so which?
3. Why has the bill received no hearing or markup in the Homeland Security
   Committee in the 18 months since referral, and has he asked for one?
4. On what basis did he describe the incident as a "terrorist attack" while
   the airline and the UAE regulator said the motive was unknown?
5. Does "our planes" in the post refer to U.S. carriers, and if so, how does
   a bill limited to U.S. public charters reach the scenario described?
