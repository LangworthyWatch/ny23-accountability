## Why this matters

Rep. Langworthy sits on the House Energy and Commerce Committee and is the
lead sponsor of the BUSES Act, a bill aimed squarely at New York City's
idling rules and its citizen-reporting program. On September 3 he posted a
video clip of the committee debate with a caption describing what the bill
does and what its opponents want. This entry checks that caption against
the committee's own recording of the same debate, the text of the bill and
the amendments, and the New York City rules the bill targets. The gap is
unusually direct: the central claim in the post is contradicted by an
amendment he thanked a Democrat for, urged colleagues to adopt, and saw
adopted, the day before he posted.

## The Facts

### What the bill says

H.R. 9317, the Buses Utilizing Safety and Environmental Standards (BUSES)
Act, was introduced June 15, 2026 by Langworthy with Rep. Josh Gottheimer
(D-NJ) as original cosponsor; Reps. Glenn Thompson and Mike Lawler joined
later. As introduced, it does three things through the Clean Air Act:

1. Bars any state implementation plan from enforcing an idling restriction
   on an over-the-road bus or a school bus "for a period of less than 15
   minutes."
2. Bars private civil actions against bus operators for such violations.
3. Bars any state or locality from maintaining "any program that provides
   monetary compensation to private individuals contingent upon the
   reporting, citation, or enforcement of engine idling violations **for
   over-the-road buses**," with existing programs "terminated" within 180
   days.

The bounty provision, even as introduced, reaches only reports on
over-the-road buses (the motorcoach definition from the Americans with
Disabilities Act). It does not mention trucks, which is what New York's
program is overwhelmingly about.

### What New York City's rules already say

New York City's idling law (Administrative Code § 24-163) sets a three-minute
limit, and one minute adjacent to a school. The same section provides that
idling of a school bus "may be permitted to the extent necessary: (1) for
mechanical work; (2) to maintain an appropriate temperature for passenger
comfort; or (3) in emergency evacuations where necessary to operate
wheelchair lifts." The Department of Environmental Protection's guidance for
the Citizens Air Complaint Program adds: "There is no violation of a bus
that idles for longer than three minutes if there are passengers actively
boarding during part of that idle period," and, after 2023 administrative
appeals, a presumption that school buses idling within 15 minutes of posted
drop-off or pick-up times adjacent to a school are maintaining passenger
comfort "and would therefore be exempt."

**In plain language:** the two scenarios in the post, helping a disabled
child board and running a wheelchair lift, are already exempt under the
city's own rules. The one scenario that is not clearly exempt is a pre-trip
engine-on safety inspection.

### What the "almost $1 million" refers to

The figure is real, but not about buses. The New York Post reported in
November 2025 that the program's top earner, Ernest Welde, "has made
$895,737 since 2019 off the program," and that another top earner made his
money "by filming trucks idling for more than 3 minutes." Complainants
receive 25 percent of a fine when filed through DEP and up to 50 percent
when filed directly with the city's administrative tribunal. In committee
the Congressman put it more carefully than his post did: "Some participants
have reportedly made hundreds of thousands of dollars in this new cottage
industry" (76:58).

**In plain language:** the version of the BUSES Act that left committee, with
his support, no longer ends New York City's program. It requires the city to
carve out fifteen minutes for three specific purposes on over-the-road
buses, and lets the city keep the program for everything else, including
trucks, which are the vast majority of complaints. The "Big Brother bounty
hunt" his post says the bill "ends" would continue under his own bill.

## Update, later on September 4: a second clip, and the "ends" claim disappears

Hours after this entry was published, the official page posted a second
71-second clip from the same markup (reel 1360137656288257), captioned:

> "NYC liberals love forcing their radical agenda on rural communities like
> NY23, including banning gas stoves, and coming for our 2nd Amendment
> rights. But the second we try to stop their asinine bus-driver bounty
> scheme that is dangerous and detached from reality, the socialists all of
> a sudden want local control. Rules for thee, not for me."

The clip is his reply at the 119-minute mark: "I'm very familiar with your
city and its laws and how bad they are and how contagious they become to
other copycats around the country. And that's why we have to take federal
action... it doesn't stop you from opining about how we should heat our
homes in my district."

Three things to note. First, the claim that his bill "ends this Big Brother
bounty hunt" does not appear in the second caption; the bill is now
something to "stop" the scheme. Second, the "local control" charge runs in
both directions in his own words: the clip argues for federal preemption of
a city ordinance ("we have to take federal action"), while the caption
objects to state policy reaching rural counties. His Energy Choice Act,
H.R. 3699, likewise uses federal authority to override state and local
building-energy rules; see [that entry](/fact-checks/2026-02-25-energy-choice-act/).
Third, "banning gas stoves" refers to New York's All-Electric Buildings Act,
which restricts natural-gas hookups in most **new** construction starting
in 2026 and 2029; it does not ban existing stoves. The Second Amendment
reference has no referent in the clip or the markup. (Transcript:
research/transcripts/2026-09-04-fb-reel-1360137656288257-buses-act-clip-2.txt;
embed capture in the archive log.)

## The fair reading

- **The safety concern is not invented.** A federal pre-trip inspection can
  require the engine running, and the DEP guidance has no explicit exemption
  for it; that is the gap the Peters amendment was drafted to close. A
  one-minute limit adjacent to a school is genuinely short.
- **He accepted a narrowing.** Agreeing to a Democratic amendment that
  preserves local enforcement is, on the merits, the more moderate course.
  The problem is that his post describes the bill he introduced, not the
  bill he voted out.
- **The bounty program has real critics beyond him.** The city's own DEP
  commissioner has acknowledged fraudulent duplicate submissions, the bus
  industry has sued, and Gottheimer, a Democrat, co-leads the bill. Whether
  paying citizens to film idling vehicles is good policy is a legitimate
  debate this entry does not join.
- **"Battled" is a fair word for the exchange.** Members on both sides
  spoke sharply; Carter's "shame on you" and Langworthy's "theatrics" reply
  are both in the record.

## Questions this raises

1. The post says the bill "ends" the bounty program. The amendment he urged
   the committee to adopt lets localities "amend their program to comply."
   Which description does his office stand behind?
2. City rules exempt school buses loading passengers and operating wheelchair
   lifts. Can his office cite a single New York City idling summons issued
   for a driver assisting a disabled child to board?
3. The bill's bounty provision, before and after amendment, reaches only
   reports on over-the-road buses. Does he intend it to affect the truck
   complaints that produce nearly all of the program's payouts?
4. His September 3 post on "ten great bills" does not mention that one is
   his own, or that it was the closest vote of the day at 26–22. Why not?
