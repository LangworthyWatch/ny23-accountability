## Why this matters

Who decides whether a jail holds someone for immigration agents matters in
NY-23 too. The state court ruling and Attorney General guidance described
below apply to sheriffs' jails across New York, including the county jails
in this district. Constituents who read that the governor is blocking
cooperation will draw conclusions about state law. This entry checks
whether the decision in this case was hers.

## The record

### What DHS says happened

The Department of Homeland Security's November 17, 2026 release says that
on April 29, 2026, "ICE lodged a detainer" for a man "after he had been
arrested in Brooklyn for the charges of attempted murder, first-degree
assault" and weapons offenses. It says "His criminal history includes a
prior arrest for third-degree assault," that he "illegally overstayed his
authorized period after it expired in January 2015," and that New York
officials "RELEASED" him "from Rikers Island back onto the streets on
November 7. He currently remains at large."

DHS describes arrests and charges. Its release does not say he has been
convicted of anything. Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin's own
statement refers to "these violent charges." Neither DHS nor the Post says
why he was released on November 7, whether by bail, a court order or
another outcome in the criminal case.

### Who declined the detainer

The Post's story, credited to Fox News, says the detainer was one "which
the New York City Department of Correction declined to honor, allowing him
to be released from Rikers Island." Rikers is run by the city's Department
of Correction, a city agency.

### The law the city follows

New York City Administrative Code §9-131, set by Local Law 58 of 2014,
governs the Department of Correction. As a federal judge summarized it on
October 15, 2026, the department "may honor a section 287.7 detainer
request only if (a) federal immigration authorities provide a 'judicial
warrant' and (b) the individual 'has been convicted of a violent or serious
crime' or 'is identified as a possible match in the terrorist screening
database.'"

Both conditions must be met. A pending charge, even a serious one, does not
meet the conviction requirement. DHS's release does not say whether ICE
presented a judicial warrant.

### What state law adds

- **A 2018 state court ruling.** In People ex rel. Wells v. DeMarco, the
  Appellate Division, Second Department, held that "New York state and
  local law enforcement officers are not authorized by New York law to
  effectuate arrests for civil law immigration violations." The Attorney
  General's February 2026 guidance says agencies "have authority to honor
  such detainers if the federal authorities present a judicial warrant."
  This ruling came in 2018, before Gov. Hochul took office in August 2021.
- **A federal court in October 2026.** Dismissing the Justice
  Department's suit against the city's detainer laws, Judge Ramón Reyes
  wrote that "even if this Court eliminated sections 9-131(b), 9-205(b),
  14-154(b) of New York City's Administrative Code, New York City officers
  would still be unable to execute a section 287.7 detainer request to
  maintain custody of a non-citizen without a judicial warrant."
- **The 2026 state law Gov. Hochul signed.** The budget law enacted this
  year includes the "Local Cops, Local Crimes Act," which bars local
  governments, police and jails from 287(g) agreements with ICE and from
  contracts to "house or detain individuals for federal civil immigration
  violations." Its separate rules on municipal employees and immigration
  detainers do not reach jails: the law's definition of "municipal
  government" says it "shall not include a local correctional facility,"
  and its definition of "municipal government employee" excludes police
  officers and peace officers. The law also states it does not bar
  compliance with federal judicial warrants and court orders.

**In plain language:** The decision not to hold him was made by New York
City's jail system, under a city law from 2014. That law requires both a
judge's warrant and a conviction, and DHS describes only charges. A federal
judge wrote last month that the city could not have held him on a detainer
without a judge's warrant even if the city law did not exist. The state law
Gov. Hochul signed this year limits local cooperation with ICE in other
ways, but it leaves local jails out of its detainer rules.

### What Gov. Hochul has said

At the August 25, 2026 announcement of the new law, Gov. Hochul said: "Once
they're convicted and do their time, we work with ICE to ensure they're
deported... New York State will never become a sanctuary for criminals." She
added that "local police will assist ICE only if a real crime has been
committed." Her spokesperson told Fox News Digital for the Post's story: "By
law, New York State always notifies federal immigration enforcement of any
non-citizen serving a State prison sentence." That statement concerns state
prisons, not city jails like Rikers.

## The fair reading

Parts of the post match what DHS says. ICE lodged a detainer, it was not
honored, the man was released from Rikers on November 7, and DHS says he is
at large and overstayed his authorized stay. Gov. Hochul did sign a 2026 law that
restricts local cooperation with ICE, and New York's courts have limited
civil immigration arrests by local officers since 2018. "NYS's sanctuary
policies" describes something real.

Three parts do not hold up. "An attempted murderer with multiple violent
felonies" describes charges as convictions; DHS lists arrests and charges.
"Because Hochul won't allow law enforcement to cooperate with ICE detainers"
assigns the decision to the governor, when the city's Department of
Correction made it under city law, a 2018 court ruling and the federal
judicial-warrant requirement a federal judge described last month, while
the state law she signed excludes local jails from its detainer rules. And
"She would rather protect THIS guy than your safety" is a claim about her
motive, which her own public statements contradict and no evidence
supports.

**Verdict: FALSE ATTRIBUTION.** The release followed a New York City jail
policy set by city law in 2014, under rules a state court set in 2018,
before she took office. The post attributes it to Gov. Hochul and describes
a man charged with crimes as convicted of them.
